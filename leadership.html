01

How I lead

I care personally and challenge directly — Radical Candor, in Kim Scott's framing. I work for my team rather than the other way around, but working for the team means setting a direction clear enough that they can push back on it, not waiting to be told where to go.

The precondition for any of that is a safe space to be creative and experiment with ideas. Nobody challenges directly in a room where being wrong is expensive.

Work for the team

The manager's job is to clear the road, not to occupy it. I'd rather be the person removing a blocker than the one approving the work.

In practice At TribalScale that meant taking the tech-feasibility conversations myself — walking blue-sky wireframes through each Platform Lead — so designers could keep exploring instead of self-censoring against constraints they'd half-guessed. On Equinox+ it meant holding the client relationship while teaching the team 10-foot practice, so they finished the engagement more capable than they started.

Pods over hierarchy

Small cross-functional pods with real decision-making authority. Consistent communication matters more than reporting lines.

In practice Pods scoped so a decision can be made inside one, not escalated out of it. The test I use: if a pod has to wait on me to move, the pod is wrong, not the designer.

Everyone at the table is a designer

Engineers, PMs, copywriters, customer service. The best insight in my career came from a customer service team nobody had asked.

In practice At Deciem, floor sales and customer service knew exactly what was broken — they were just never in the room. Getting them into the prioritization exercise changed what the whole company built next.

Advocacy is how design earns its seat

Design advocacy isn't self-promotion. It's the work of getting design into the conversation early enough to change the question, not just the answer.

In practice Scoping and proposal work at Renga, teaching engineering and product partners the vocabulary, and taking research findings to stakeholders unfiltered rather than summarised into agreement.

Above all: listen, be kind, never stop learning, stay aware of my own positionality, and choose diversity over monoculture.

02

DesignOps

My specialty is building design teams — which in practice means designing the operations that let a team scale without losing its judgment.

That covers hiring and onboarding, the rituals that keep quality consistent as headcount grows, and the harder structural work of bridging discovery and strategy so design isn't handed a solved problem to decorate.

Where I've done this

  • Renga, as VP of Design — grew the practice from one designer to a team of six designers and three strategists, building the DesignOps to support it as the company scaled.
  • TribalScale, as Design Manager — mentored and grew a design team of twelve across web, mobile, TV, voice and automotive client work.
  • Deciem, as consultant — built the research and prioritization practice that gave five departments a shared view of the customer.

03

Moving design maturity

Most of my career has been spent moving organizations one or two steps along the same ladder.

The pattern repeats across sectors: design arrives as a service, proves it can be trusted with a problem rather than a ticket, and eventually gets to set the question. The work of a design leader is largely the work of moving an org up this ladder — and knowing which step it's actually on, not which step it says it's on.

The diagnostic I use

  • Who writes the problem statement — design, or someone handing design a solution?
  • When does design enter — at discovery, or after scoping is locked?
  • Does research change roadmaps, or just validate them?
  • Can a designer say no, and does the org survive it?
Level 01
Design as service
Design receives tickets. Quality is judged on execution against a spec written elsewhere.
Level 02
Design as partner
Design is in the room at scoping. Research exists but rarely changes direction.
Level 03
Design as strategy
Design frames the problem. Research redirects roadmaps. Systems and standards are owned, not requested.
Level 04
Design as direction
Design sets vision alongside product and engineering, and the org designs for futures rather than quarters.
Fig. 01 The maturity ladder I diagnose against, adapted from the Nielsen Norman Group and InVision design maturity models. Most of my engagements have started at level one or two; the interesting work is the move to three, and the reason I went back for a foresight degree is level four.

05

Why foresight

I went back for an MDes in Strategic Foresight and Innovation because I kept hitting the ceiling of what user research can tell you. Research describes the present accurately. It does not tell a team where to go next.

CLA
Causal Layered Analysis — getting past the litany to the worldview holding a problem in place
3H
Three Horizons — naming the current system, the transition, and the preferred future distinctly
Leverage
Meadows' intervention points — where in a system a change actually holds

These are the tools I now use to set direction for a team: not to predict, but to make the assumptions in a roadmap visible enough to argue with. It's the same instinct that made me good at systems work — the belief that if you only fix what's visible, it comes back.
