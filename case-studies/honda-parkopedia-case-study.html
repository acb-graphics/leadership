Honda × Parkopedia

Reserving a parking space is three different products depending on whether the car is approaching, moving, or stopped.

Client
Honda R&D Innovations
Role
Lead Product Designer
Duration
2017
Product
CarPlay & Android Auto — concept

Honda R&D Innovations wanted a proof of concept for finding and paying for parking from inside the vehicle, built on their auto platforms in partnership with Parkopedia.

01

Overview

I led flow architecture, in-vehicle interface design, and the interactive prototype for a concept evaluating both a product direction and a partnership.

The contribution I would point to is the reframing. Scoped as execution against existing designs, the work turned a partial happy-path concept into a complete definition of the problem.

Team, tools & scope

  • Scope. 3 driving states modeled · 2 platforms' payment-security models resolved · error states across both interaction lanes.
  • Tools. Sketch for flows and mockups; InVision for the interactive prototype.
  • Methods. Design audit, driving-state modeling, technical discovery, competitive review, prototyping.
02

Problem

The existing designs covered the version of the task where everything goes right. In a vehicle, very little of the real experience looks like that.

Honda R&D Innovations was evaluating whether in-vehicle parking payment was a viable direction and whether Parkopedia was the right partner. A payment transaction inside a moving vehicle is a safety question before it is a UX question.

Challenges

  • Error states had not yet been designed, leaving no response at the moments carrying the most risk.
  • The in-motion state sat outside the original scope, though it is a substantially different design problem.
  • Payment security behavior across CarPlay and Android Auto was still an open question at the start.
03

Research

Discovery was a design audit, a competitive review, and a technical investigation. No driver research was run.

I analyzed Honda's existing work against the full set of user states, which is how the two gaps surfaced. A competitive review of automotive systems and parking apps showed how much of the category assumed a stationary user with a phone in hand.

What the research revealed

  • Driving state is the organizing variable. The same task splits into three products depending on whether the driver is approaching, moving, or stopped.
  • Platform constraints define what is designable. Stored payment behavior differed across the two platforms, so the flow was built to the stricter.
  • The happy path was the smallest part of the problem. Most of the experience lives in states the original designs had not yet reached.
Fig. 01 The existing registered-user flow, mapped against approaching, in-motion, and parked states — the audit that surfaced the two missing pieces: error handling and the in-motion lane.
04

Process

  1. 01

    Audit before execution. Mapped the existing designs against the full set of user states, which reframed the engagement from execution into problem definition.

  2. 02

    Separate the driving states. Split the experience into approaching, in motion, and parked — voice-driven confirmation while moving, touch-driven search and payment when stopped.

  3. 03

    Resolve platform constraints early. Established how each platform handles stored payment methods, with the development team, before design was committed.

  4. 04

    Prototype for prioritization. Built an interactive prototype the team used to sequence features, turning a concept review into a build plan.

Decisions and rationale. The load-bearing decision was designing for the most constrained context first. Building the in-motion lane ahead of the parked one — voice confirmation, minimal visual load, no free-text entry — allowed the parked experience to be richer without the two diverging into separate products.

05

Solution

A complete in-vehicle parking concept covering three driving states, with a payment flow designed against what CarPlay and Android Auto actually permit.

In motion, the interface surfaces nearby lots and accepts voice confirmation, with no map manipulation or payment entry. Parked, it opens up to map search, price and ETA comparison, and touch-confirmed payment. The handoff between the two is the product.

Interface decisions. The screen was reduced to what makes the decision — price, ETA, and a garage or street toggle — with reserve and navigate as the only primary actions.

What was delivered

  • A state model covering approaching, in-motion, and parked contexts, each with its own interaction pattern.
  • A payment flow designed against both platforms' security constraints, with error states specified rather than deferred.
Fig. 02 The parked-state interface, reduced to price, ETA, and a garage-or-street toggle, with reserve and navigate as the only primary actions.
06

Impact and results

Honda stakeholders approved both the concept and the Parkopedia partnership.

Business outcome. The engagement resolved the payment-security question the concept depended on and defined the in-motion and error states, giving Honda a buildable definition of the problem and an evidence-backed partnership decision.

The work concluded at proof-of-concept stage and makes no claim on any Honda parking feature released since. Parkopedia later launched in-vehicle parking payment with Honda in mainland China, through a different integration partner and on a different head unit, several years after this engagement.

What shipped, what didn't

  • The flows, interface design, and prototype were approved as the basis for sprint prioritization.
  • Nothing reached drivers under this engagement.

What worked

Declining to design forward from an incomplete map. Auditing the existing work and naming what was missing reframed the engagement from execution to definition.

What I'd do differently

Put the in-motion flow in front of drivers, even roughly. A static rig or simulator would have tested whether voice confirmation is genuinely glanceable or only appears that way at a desk — the gap I would close first.

What I learned

Context is a design constraint with more force than any brand or platform requirement. Designing for the most constrained situation first is what let this concept survive contact with engineering.
