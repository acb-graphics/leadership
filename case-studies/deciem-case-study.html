Case Study — Cross-Functional Research & Experience Strategy

Deciem

Five teams held five versions of the customer. The ones furthest from customers had the most influence over the experience.

Client
Deciem | The Abnormal Beauty Company (The Ordinary, NIOD, Hylamide)
Role
Senior UX Consultant
Started
2019
Duration

Deciem was fluent in its own product strategy — ingredients, pricing philosophy, growth plan across a portfolio of brands. What it didn't have was any clear read on what customers thought, brand by brand or about the umbrella as a whole.

01

Project overview

Deciem hired me as its first Lead Researcher and UX Strategist, with responsibility for research and strategy from the outset.

I led every research activity, defined the strategy, owned participant recruitment, and built the feature-prioritization framework, then directed the initial experience design and prototyping. I worked across five departments without formal authority over any of them, which shaped every methodological choice on the project.

Team, tools & scope

  • Team. Sole lead across research, strategy, and prototyping.
  • Tools. Google Analytics, Spark AR Studio, Figma.
  • Methods. 1:1 departmental interviews, card sorting, analytics-led usability scripting, AR concept prototyping.
  • Scope. 5 departments · 3 brands under one umbrella · 2 Spark AR concepts.
02

Problem statement

Customer service heard the frustration daily. Marketing and design had far less direct exposure to it.

Business context. Deciem was growing fast across a portfolio of brands with distinct identities, and had two live strategic questions — product education and sustainability — that it wanted answered beyond physical retail. Each team was solving a real problem, but a different one, and none of them were connected to the product roadmap.

Why it mattered. 94% of the contacts customers made to the happiness team were requests for product guidance they couldn't find themselves — and that evidence typically reached the teams least involved in deciding what to build. This was an organizational problem wearing a design problem's clothes: any redesign would have been overwritten within a quarter unless the five teams first agreed on what was actually wrong.

Why it had stalled

  • Strategy ahead of structured insight. Growth was guided by strong internal conviction, with only evolving access to a structured view of customer perspective per brand.
  • Five teams, five versions of the truth. Each department formed its own perspective, and the teams furthest from customers often had the most influence over strategic decisions.
  • No research infrastructure to build on. The research practice and the alignment it needed to produce had to be built at the same time — process and outcome moving together.
03

Research and discovery

Two research tracks ran at once: internal interviews surfaced where the five departments actually disagreed, while external research focused on the screens analytics had already flagged as failing.

Internal. I held 1:1 sessions with each department, run separately so each team's account wasn't moderated by the presence of the others.

External. Customer sessions were split into 30 minutes of Q&A on shopping behavior and sustainability, and 30 minutes of usability testing. A handful of participants aged 24–64 took part.

Data and evidence. The regimen guide drew over a million page views with a 46% bounce rate. That number was written directly into the usability script, and a second signal confirmed it: 94% of happiness-team contact was guidance customers couldn't find themselves.

Key findings

  • The influence gap was the finding. Teams shaping the brand had no exposure to what customer service heard every day — which reframed the project from a digital-experience effort into an alignment one.
  • Regimen pages failed the non-expert. Customers unfamiliar with active ingredients sometimes couldn't follow the reasoning behind Deciem's recommended combinations.
  • Sustainability interest outpaced infrastructure. Deciem ran zero-waste boxes in stores but had nothing online to support that same behavior.

04

Design process

  1. 01

    Interviewed all five departments individually to surface the real disagreement. Running the sessions separately was deliberate: in a joint session the teams with the most authority would have set the terms, and the account I needed was the one from the teams with the least.

  2. 02

    Built a prioritization framework and ran card sorting to force cross-team consensus. Turned each customer pain point into a card and had teams sort them live. When priorities differed — marketing wanted campaign visibility, customer service wanted product clarity — I ran the discussion until both sides could see how their priority mapped onto actual customer feedback. Influence came from participation, not from my authority.

  3. 03

    Used analytics to decide what to test, rather than guessing. Pulled drop-off data to identify the highest-friction screens and wrote them into the usability script, so limited qualitative time was spent only on proven problem areas.

  4. 04

    Tested three regimen-page variants against the questions a non-expert actually has. Each variant phrased the recommendation differently — by skin type, by ingredient function, by routine — to find which framing let someone unfamiliar with actives understand why they'd been given that combination.

  5. 05

    Prototyped two Spark AR concepts to solve product education where customers already were. Built Virtual Foundation Matching with the customer happiness team, calibrated by the people who handled the products daily, and a Terracycle filter that extends the in-store zero-waste program online by identifying a bottle and routing it to the correct recycling stream.

Decisions and rationale

The decision that shaped the outcome most was presenting unfiltered interview footage rather than a summarized findings deck. Footage removed a room's ability to argue with the interpretation — the teams that had never spoken to a customer watched one, and the discussion started from what was said rather than from whether I'd read it correctly.

What got in the way

No formal authority over any of the five departments, and no existing research practice to plug into. Card sorting resolved both structurally: it made the teams the authors of the priority list, so the resulting roadmap carried shared ownership rather than resting on my say-so.

05

Solution

A two-tier prioritization roadmap that split immediate fixes from larger scope-of-work items, plus two Spark AR concepts aimed at the brand's two live strategic questions.

Key features. Virtual Foundation Matching addressed product education by letting someone see a shade on their own face, calibrated by the team handling the products daily. The Terracycle filter addressed sustainability by identifying a bottle through the camera and routing it to the correct recycling stream, turning an abstract commitment into a single action a customer could take at the bin.

Why Spark AR. The platform choice was the strategy — Deciem's customers already used it, which removed the acquisition step a standalone app or a new site section would have required.

Design system elements. The regimen-page testing produced a component library that the internal team kept using after the engagement ended.

What was delivered

  • A shared, cross-departmental understanding of positioning and customer need, replacing five competing versions.
  • A prioritization roadmap separating quick wins from larger scope-of-work.
  • Two AR concepts addressing product education and sustainability beyond physical retail.

06

Impact and results

Five departments aligned on one prioritized view of customer need, and a two-tier roadmap gave leadership a sequencing decision instead of an undifferentiated backlog.

Business outcome. The personalized regimen builder shipped on theordinary.com as a direct result of the research and roadmap I delivered; I owned the prioritization and feature requirements that guided its development. Deciem's 2020 goals for the regimen guide — conversion up to 5%, bounce down to 30% — were met. I can't attribute those metrics to this work alone, but the roadmap and research were key inputs as the team worked toward those targets.

Did the AR concepts ship? No. Both stayed at concept stage. Business priorities shifted toward core product improvements, and the technical work needed to meet Deciem's quality standards took longer than the engagement's timeline allowed.

What shipped, what didn't

  • The personalized regimen builder launched — the clearest outcome of the research.
  • Both Spark AR concepts remained concepts, deprioritized as the business shifted focus and resources to other initiatives.

What worked

Making the research undeniable rather than persuasive. Card sorting let teams build the priority list themselves, and unfiltered interview footage removed the room's ability to argue with the interpretation.

What I'd do differently

I'd negotiate a clear attribution model before the research started, rather than relying on company-wide analytics after the fact. I'd also widen the sample beyond Toronto — a portfolio sold internationally deserves a geographically and demographically broader read, even if that means shorter sessions across more markets.

What I learned

The gap in most organizations isn't a lack of customer insight — it's that the insight sits with the teams that have the least power to act on it. Customer service knew the problems already; my job was giving that evidence a route into the rooms where roadmaps get set.
