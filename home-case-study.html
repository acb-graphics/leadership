Case Study — 0→1 Product Strategy, Research & Brand

Home

A founder had conviction and a concept. In eight weeks we found out whether anyone else wanted it.

Client
Home (early-stage fintech — savings + advice for first-time homebuyers)
Engagement
Renga
Duration
8 weeks, kickoff to final shareout
Year

A founder came to us with conviction and a concept: a product that would combine savings and advice so first-time buyers could actually get to a down payment. What they didn't have was evidence.

01

Project overview

Sole designer-researcher on an eight-week engagement, reporting directly to the client-founder.

I owned research design, discovery, concept testing, UX and prototyping end to end, and co-led brand and positioning workshops with the Renga team. Because there was one designer and no sequencing room, the accountability was unusually flat: every research decision, design decision, and recommendation to the founder was mine to make and defend.

Team, tools & scope

  • Team.
  • Tools.
  • Methods. Quantitative survey with journey-stage segmentation, video interviews recruited from the survey pool, four facilitated positioning workshops, competitive landscape mapping, prototype concept testing.
  • Scope. 410 survey responses collected and segmented · 6 home-buying journey stages mapped · brand, positioning, prototype and roadmap delivered as one package.
02

Problem statement

No product, no validated market, and no definition of what a first release should even contain.

Business context. The idea had momentum but nothing underneath it to hand to investors or engineers. The founder needed two things the concept alone couldn't produce: evidence credible enough to raise against, and a scope definition specific enough to hire against.

Why it mattered. Building without validation would have spent the founder's runway proving an assumption rather than testing it. The engagement's real job was to make the next decision — build, pivot, or stop — an informed one.

Why it had stalled

  • No validation. Market fit was assumed, not tested — and nothing could responsibly be built until that assumption was checked.
  • No MVP definition. There was a feature wishlist, but no shared view of what the minimum viable product actually was or which problem it solved first.
  • No room to be sequential. Strategy, research, brand, positioning, and concept testing all had to happen inside a single 8-week window, in parallel, with one designer-researcher.

User pain points

First-time buyers were the assumed user, and the survey and interviews were designed to establish what actually blocked them rather than to confirm the founder's version.

03

Research and discovery

A 410-response survey, segmented by stage in the home-buying journey, then converted into a qualitative pipeline.

Methods. I fielded a quantitative survey that sorted respondents by where they were in the buying journey, then recruited video-interview participants from within that same pool. That meant concept testing spoke to real buyers at the exact stage each concept targeted, rather than to a cold-recruited panel standing in for them.

Segments, not personas. The organizing frame was journey stage — from early saving through to selling — rather than demographic persona. Stage predicted need far more reliably than age or income, and it was also the axis the competitive analysis turned on.

Key findings

  • Nearly every competitor owned a single stage. Plotting the market against the six stages from saving to selling exposed that no one was carrying a buyer across the whole journey — which became the strategic wedge and the anchor slide of the investor deck.

04

Design process

  1. 01

    Structured an 8-week plan that front-loaded evidence. Sequenced strategy, research, positioning, and concept testing so validation gated design work, rather than running design and research in parallel and hoping they'd agree. This was the first real decision of the engagement and the one everything else depended on.

  2. 02

    Facilitated four positioning workshops to force decisions. Ran four 1.5-hour sessions with the founder to lock mission, positioning, tone and voice — converting a fuzzy idea into language the business could actually be built and pitched on.

  3. 03

    Mapped the competitive landscape across the entire home-buying journey. Plotted competitors against the six stages from saving to selling, which is what exposed the single-stage pattern and gave the positioning something defensible to sit on.

  4. 04

    Turned one research instrument into two. Rather than running a survey and then recruiting separately, I built the interview pipeline out of the survey pool — the highest-leverage decision of the project, and the reason concept testing was sharper than a cold-recruited panel would have allowed in the time available.

  5. 05

    Built and tested a prototype the founder could hand to a team. Designed the onboarding and savings-goal flow, tested it in interviews across each buying stage, and paired it with a roadmap that translated "what the founder wanted to build" into the problem worth solving first.

Testing and iteration

The prototype was tested with interview participants at each stage of the buying journey.

What got in the way

Eight weeks, one designer-researcher, and five workstreams that all wanted to be first. The constraint was resolved by refusing to run them concurrently — gating design behind validation meant the schedule was tight but never circular, and it meant no design work had to be thrown away when the evidence arrived.

05

Solution

A validated product direction delivered as one package: brand and positioning, a tested onboarding and savings-goal prototype, a waitlist landing page, and a roadmap the client could execute without us.

Key features. The core of the product experience was a short question flow that personalised a savings goal to the individual buyer — the mechanism that made "savings plus advice" concrete rather than conceptual. The waitlist page carried the positioning into market before any product existed, so the messaging could be tested against real interest.

06

Impact and results

The client hired a permanent design and engineering team off the back of this work — the outcome that matters most, because it means the direction was credible enough to staff against.

Business outcome. The brand and competitive landscape became the spine of the investor deck. The waitlist page converted real interest before a product existed, giving the founder a demand signal to show investors rather than a projection.

User feedback.

Measurement gap

  • The waitlist page generated a demand signal that was observed rather than instrumented. The signup count and the fundraising outcome are the two figures this case study needs, and both are pending confirmation rather than estimated.

What worked

Refusing to design before there was evidence. The founder arrived knowing what they wanted to build; the survey and interviews reframed the engagement around what problem was actually worth solving, and that reframe is what made the roadmap credible enough to hire against. Recruiting interview participants out of the survey pool was the highest-leverage decision of the project — it turned one research instrument into two and made concept testing sharper than a cold-recruited panel would have been.

What I'd do differently

I'd pair the network-sourced survey with a screened panel to check whether the segment patterns held outside my own circle — fast and free is a real advantage at eight weeks, but it isn't a defensible sample. I'd also instrument the waitlist page deliberately from day one. It produced the engagement's clearest demand signal and I can describe it but not quantify it, which is exactly the evidence an investor conversation leans on hardest.

What I learned

In a 0→1 engagement, the deliverable isn't the prototype — it's the client's ability to continue without you. The measure of this project wasn't the screens; it was that the founder could write job descriptions, brief a team, and defend the direction in a room I wasn't in.
