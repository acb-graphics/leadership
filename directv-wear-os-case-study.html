AT&T had a DirecTV mobile app and a set-top box that customers already lived with. What it wanted next was the wrist — a Wear OS companion that would let someone browse live TV, play, and record from a watch.

01

Situation

The ambition wasn't a smaller version of the phone app; it was continuity.

Start something on one device, pick it up on another, never repeat a step.

Why it was hard

  • Three surfaces, one state. Set-top box, mobile app, and watch had to stay in sync in near real time, or the continuity promise broke immediately.
  • No tolerance for repeated steps. An action begun on one device couldn't force the user to redo it elsewhere — which pushed complexity out of the interface and into the handoff logic between devices.
  • The remote metaphor didn't survive the wrist. Wear OS's screen size and input model meant most of what makes a TV remote work simply couldn't translate, so the interaction model had to be rebuilt rather than adapted.
02

Objective

Ship a Wear OS companion app that behaves as one part of a connected system rather than a standalone product — and prove the concept convincingly enough for AT&T to commit to the wearable category.

My role: Contributed. Senior Interactive Designer on the Accedo team — I owned the device-contingency logic with engineering and the high-fidelity UI and motion guide, working within a team on flow architecture.

Timeframe: To be confirmed.

What success looked like

  • A released Wear OS app syncing live TV browsing, playback, and recording with the set-top box and mobile app.
  • Complete flow coverage for both at-home and out-of-home use, including the failure states — no receiver paired, receiver offline, multiple receivers in a household.
  • A technically viable interaction model, validated against real Wear OS and device-communication constraints rather than designed in the abstract.
  • Stakeholder sign-off at AT&T on something they could see working, not a static deck.
03

Actions

  1. 01

    Worked directly with engineering to define contingency messaging from device behavior. Sat with developers to understand how the devices actually communicated, then designed what the watch shows when a receiver is offline, unpaired, or mid-buffer — turning technical failure modes into intelligible user-facing states.

  2. 02

    Mapped the experience across two distinct contexts rather than one generic user. Separated at-home from out-of-home: remote-control parity from the wrist in one, live-show notifications with remote recording to a chosen set-top box in the other — a split that determined the whole information architecture.

  3. 03

    Designed the pairing and setup flows that made multi-receiver households work. Covered watch-to-network joining, receiver discovery, IP validation, and default-receiver selection, so a household with several set-top boxes could record to a specific room ("Record Westworld in the Living Room") instead of guessing.

  4. 04

    Built a working demo to get stakeholder sign-off. Converted wireframes into a functional demonstration showing the same title in sync across the living-room TV and the watch — which is what actually moved AT&T to approve, where static wireframes wouldn't have.

  5. 05

    Produced a high-fidelity UI and motion guide, and ran the copy handoff. Labeled every screen where copy was still needed and worked with AT&T's writing team to hold brand voice consistent across three devices with very different amounts of room for language.

04

Impact

Shipped a released Wear OS app connecting set-top box, mobile app, and watch into one synced system — live TV browsing, playback, and recording from the wrist.

A demo that secured stakeholder sign-off on a technically uncertain concept, converting an internal debate into an approved build.

What it delivered

  • Design specification that accounted for real device-communication failure modes, not just the happy path — the difference between a concept and something engineering could ship.
  • AT&T's product team committed to Apple Watch OS next, on the strength of this app's reception — the clearest available signal that the work validated the wearable category for them internally.

05

Reflection

What worked

Designing the failure states alongside engineering rather than after them. On a three-device system, the offline and unpaired cases aren't edge cases — they're most of the actual experience, and treating them as first-class from discovery is why the flows held up. Building a working demo instead of presenting wireframes was the other decision that mattered: continuity across devices is something people have to see behaving, not read about, and the sign-off came in the room because of it.

What I'd refine

The work was validated technically and with stakeholders, but not with customers. I'd have pushed for even lightweight testing on the wrist before release — Wear OS interaction assumptions are exactly the kind that feel obvious in a demo and fail in a kitchen. I'd also want the post-launch measurement agreed before shipping. The app went to market and informed a platform decision, but the evidence behind that decision lives in a feedback conversation rather than in numbers I can point to.

What I learned

In a multi-device system, the design object isn't any of the screens — it's the handoff between them. The hardest decisions on this project were about what state travels, what gets remembered, and what a device does when it can't reach the others. That reframing has shaped how I scope connected-product work since.
