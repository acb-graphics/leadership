AT&T had a DirecTV mobile app and a set-top box that customers already lived with. What it wanted next was the wrist — a Wear OS companion that would let someone browse live TV, play, and record from a watch.

01

Project overview

Contributing designer on the Accedo team, owning the device-contingency logic with engineering and the high-fidelity UI and motion guide.

This was a shared-ownership project rather than a solo lead one, and the page says so — flow architecture was worked within a team. What was mine end to end was the failure-state model: what the watch shows when a receiver is offline, unpaired, or mid-buffer.

Team, tools & scope

  • Team. Accedo design and engineering, plus AT&T's product and writing teams.
  • Tools.
  • Methods. Engineering-led contingency modelling, context-based flow architecture, pairing and setup design, working demo for stakeholder sign-off, motion specification, cross-team copy handoff.
  • Scope. 3 connected surfaces — set-top box, mobile app, watch · 2 usage contexts — at-home and out-of-home · pairing, receiver discovery, IP validation and default-receiver selection flows · full failure-state coverage.
02

Problem statement

The ambition wasn't a smaller version of the phone app; it was continuity. Start something on one device, pick it up on another, never repeat a step.

Business context. AT&T was evaluating the wearable category. The Wear OS app had to work as a product and as an argument — good enough to justify further investment in a platform the business hadn't committed to.

Why it mattered. A companion app that breaks continuity is worse than no companion app: it adds a device to the household without removing a step from the task. The promise only holds if state travels reliably, which made the engineering constraints the design constraints.

Why it was hard

  • Three surfaces, one state. Set-top box, mobile app, and watch had to stay in sync in near real time, or the continuity promise broke immediately.
  • No tolerance for repeated steps. An action begun on one device couldn't force the user to redo it elsewhere — which pushed complexity out of the interface and into the handoff logic between devices.
  • The remote metaphor didn't survive the wrist. Wear OS's screen size and input model meant most of what makes a TV remote work simply couldn't translate, so the interaction model had to be rebuilt rather than adapted.

User pain points

Two distinct contexts produce two distinct problems. At home, the pain is the remote — misplaced, shared, or across the room. Out of home, the pain is missing something: a live show starts and there's no way to record it to the right box without going back to a phone. Multi-receiver households compound both, because "record this" is ambiguous until you can name a room.

These were derived from AT&T's product understanding and the existing DirecTV app rather than from primary research with customers. No user research was run on this engagement.

03

Research and discovery

Discovery here was technical rather than user-facing: understanding how the three devices actually communicated, and designing from what the system could truthfully report.

Engineering discovery. I sat with developers to establish how the set-top box, phone and watch exchanged state — what each device could know about the others, how quickly, and what happened when the link dropped. That investigation produced the contingency model, and it's the reason the failure states are specific rather than generic.

Platform constraints. Wear OS's input model and screen size were treated as a starting point rather than an obstacle: the question was what a watch is genuinely good for in this system, not how to compress a remote onto it.

User research: none. The work was validated technically and with AT&T stakeholders. Neither is customer validation, and for a wrist interface — where assumptions feel obvious in a demo and fail in a kitchen — that's a real limitation.

Competitive analysis.

Key findings

  • Failure states aren't edge cases here. On a three-device system, offline, unpaired and mid-buffer conditions are a large share of the actual experience, which is why they were designed first rather than last.
  • Context splits the product in two. At-home wants remote-control parity; out-of-home wants notification and remote recording. Treating them as one generic user would have produced an interface that served neither.
  • Multi-receiver households need naming. Recording is only unambiguous if a user can direct it to a specific room, which made default-receiver selection a first-class part of setup rather than a settings afterthought.
04

Design process

  1. 01

    Worked directly with engineering to define contingency messaging from device behaviour. Designed what the watch shows when a receiver is offline, unpaired, or mid-buffer — turning technical failure modes into intelligible user-facing states rather than error codes.

  2. 02

    Mapped the experience across two distinct contexts rather than one generic user. Separated at-home from out-of-home: remote-control parity from the wrist in one, live-show notifications with remote recording to a chosen set-top box in the other — a split that determined the whole information architecture.

  3. 03

    Designed the pairing and setup flows that made multi-receiver households work. Covered watch-to-network joining, receiver discovery, IP validation, and default-receiver selection, so a household with several set-top boxes could record to a specific room — "Record Westworld in the Living Room" — instead of guessing.

  4. 04

    Built a working demo to get stakeholder sign-off. Converted wireframes into a functional demonstration showing the same title in sync across the living-room TV and the watch — which is what actually moved AT&T to approve, where static wireframes wouldn't have.

  5. 05

    Produced a high-fidelity UI and motion guide, and ran the copy handoff. Labelled every screen where copy was still needed and worked with AT&T's writing team to hold brand voice consistent across three devices with very different amounts of room for language.

Decisions and rationale

Designing the failure states alongside engineering rather than after them was the decision the project rests on. The alternative — design the happy path, then ask engineering what could go wrong — produces error screens that describe system conditions rather than user situations. Starting from device behaviour meant every contingency message answered "what should I do now" instead of "what went wrong."

Testing and iteration

Iteration ran against engineering feasibility and stakeholder review, not against users. The working demo was the mechanism: continuity across devices is something people have to see behaving, and reviewing a live sync rather than a static flow is what surfaced the timing and state questions early.

05

Solution

A Wear OS companion that behaves as one part of a connected system — live TV browsing, playback, and recording from the wrist, in sync with the set-top box and mobile app.

Key interactions. At home, the watch acts as a remote with parity for the actions that make sense on a wrist — browse, play, record — without attempting the full remote's surface. Out of home, live-show notifications arrive on the wrist and can be recorded to a named receiver in a single confirmation. Pairing covers network joining, receiver discovery, IP validation and default-receiver selection, so the "which room" question is answered once at setup rather than every time.

Failure states as features. No receiver paired, receiver offline, multiple receivers in a household, mid-buffer — each has a designed state that tells the user what to do next. On a three-device system these carry more of the experience than the happy path does.

Design system and motion. A high-fidelity UI and motion guide covered the transitions and states, specified for a screen where motion carries wayfinding that layout can't.

Copy. Every screen needing copy was labelled and routed to AT&T's writing team, holding one brand voice across three devices with very different amounts of room for language.

What was delivered

  • A released Wear OS app syncing live TV browsing, playback, and recording with the set-top box and mobile app.
  • Complete flow coverage for both at-home and out-of-home use, including the failure states.
  • A technically viable interaction model validated against real Wear OS and device-communication constraints.
  • A high-fidelity UI and motion guide, plus a structured copy handoff.

06

Impact and results

The app shipped, and AT&T's product team committed to Apple Watch OS next on the strength of its reception.

Business outcome. The clearest evidence the work landed is a platform decision: AT&T went from evaluating the wearable category to investing further in it. That signal is real but it's a decision rather than a number, and it lives in a feedback conversation rather than in instrumentation I can point to.

User feedback.

Measurement gap

  • No adoption, retention or usage figure was agreed before launch, so the app's performance is described rather than measured. On a project whose explicit purpose was to prove a category, that's the evidence that would have made the argument for AT&T rather than to them.

What worked

Designing the failure states alongside engineering rather than after them. On a three-device system, the offline and unpaired cases aren't edge cases — they're most of the actual experience, and treating them as first-class from discovery is why the flows held up. Building a working demo instead of presenting wireframes was the other decision that mattered: continuity across devices is something people have to see behaving, not read about, and the sign-off came in the room because of it.

What I'd do differently

I'd push for even lightweight testing on the wrist before release. Wear OS interaction assumptions are exactly the kind that feel obvious in a demo and fail in a kitchen, and nothing in this process would have caught that. I'd also fix the post-launch measurement before shipping — the app informed a platform decision, and I'd want that decision resting on numbers rather than on impressions, both for the client's sake and for my own ability to describe the outcome.

What I learned

In a multi-device system, the design object isn't any of the screens — it's the handoff between them. The hardest decisions on this project were about what state travels, what gets remembered, and what a device does when it can't reach the others. That reframing has shaped how I scope connected-product work since.
