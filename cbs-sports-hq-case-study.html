CBS Sports was launching CBS Sports HQ — a free, 24/7 streaming sports news channel — and needed a product experience to carry it across TV and mobile, built while the broadcast team was still testing the channel itself.

01

Project overview

Design lead across all four platforms, negotiating feature scope directly with each Tech Platform Lead.

The accountability that defined this role wasn't the screens — it was the scope. Four surfaces with different capabilities on one launch date usually ends in either a lowest-common-denominator product or a slipped date, and avoiding both was the job.

Team, tools & scope

  • Team. Four Tech Platform Leads, plus the TribalScale engagement team and CBS Sports' broadcast and product organisations.
  • Tools.
  • Methods. Analytics-informed problem framing, competitive format analysis (live TV news), blue-sky concepting, platform-by-platform feasibility negotiation, full-state specification, post-launch iteration from early-adopter behaviour.
  • Scope. 4 platforms shipped on launch day — phone and tablet, Fire TV, Apple TV, Roku, plus web · 3 connected-TV platforms refined post-launch · new and returning user states specified across the full connected-TV experience.
02

Problem statement

The existing CBS Sports surfing experience wasn't working: too much content, too many entry points, and a drop-off rate that pointed straight at cognitive overload.

Business context. CBS Sports HQ was a free, ad-supported, 24/7 channel — a bet on reach. The product experience had to make live viewing the default while keeping the existing on-demand library findable, and it had to ship on the broadcast team's launch date, inside CBS Sports' existing architecture.

Why it mattered. A free 24/7 channel only works if people stay. Drop-off was the metric the brief was written against, which made this one of the rare projects where the problem arrived already quantified.

Why it was hard

  • No clean slate. Everything had to be built within CBS Sports' existing architecture, so the design was constrained by decisions made long before the project started.
  • The existing experience was losing people. High drop-off from content overload meant the problem wasn't adding features — it was subtracting enough to make live viewing legible.
  • Four platforms, four sets of constraints, one launch date. TV, laptop, tablet, and phone had to ship as one connected structure, in parallel with the broadcast team's own launch, with each platform capable of a different amount.

User pain points

The core pain point was choice paralysis at the entry point: too many surfaces competing for the first decision, with no clear default. The second was a control problem that only became visible after launch — a full-screen video on the home page gave users no sense of what else was navigable, so the experience read as a dead end rather than a starting point.

03

Research and discovery

No primary user research was run before launch. Discovery was analytics, format analysis, and platform feasibility — and the most important finding arrived after shipping, from real early adopters.

Data and evidence. The brief was anchored in an existing drop-off rate, which is what allowed the problem to be diagnosed as content density rather than missing features.

Competitive and format analysis. I studied how live TV news channels keep a broadcast running while surfacing other stories — an existing solved problem in an adjacent medium, and the source of the picture-in-picture decision.

Technical discovery. Feasibility was established platform by platform with each Tech Platform Lead, walking blue-sky concepts and wireframes through against real capability rather than assumed parity.

Post-launch signal. Early-adopter behaviour and feedback on the Apple TV MVP drove the home-page revision that followed.

Key findings

  • The problem was density, not absence. Reframing the brief from "surface more content" to "make live legible" was the structural decision everything else followed from.
  • Live news had already solved discovery-without-interruption. The format keeps a broadcast running while other stories surface around it — directly transferable to a 24/7 sports channel.
  • Full-screen video removed the sense of control. Early adopters couldn't tell what else was navigable, which is what prompted the collapsed-video revision.
The pre-launch work was validated against platform leads and stakeholders, not against users. Stakeholder sign-off is not user validation, and the distinction matters here: the change that mattered most came from customers after shipping, when it was most expensive to make.
04

Design process

  1. 01

    Diagnosed the drop-off as a content-density problem, then designed subtraction. Reframed the brief from "surface more content" to "make live legible" — the structural decision that everything else followed from.

  2. 02

    Concepted blue-sky, then negotiated scope platform by platform. Walked concepts and wireframes through with every Tech Platform Lead individually, scaling features down against each platform's real capabilities — which kept four surfaces on one launch date instead of one platform holding the rest hostage.

  3. 03

    Borrowed the live-news format to solve discoverability without sacrificing "always on." Landed on picture-in-picture as the mechanism that let live content stay visible while a user browsed — resolving the live-versus-discovery tension structurally rather than through navigation.

  4. 04

    Specified every state across the connected TV experience, for both new and returning users. Stitched together the full scenario set rather than the happy path — the work that made the experience shippable inside an existing architecture.

  5. 05

    Drove the post-launch revision that changed the home page. Used early-adopter feedback to move the home page from full-screen video to a collapsed video — giving users a clearer sense of what else was navigable — and carried that refinement into Roku and Fire TV.

Decisions and rationale

Two concepts were taken to the platform leads. The first kept a full-screen live game with a sidebar and a "top videos" strip; the second made the live module a persistent element with a shelf beneath it. The second won because it degraded better — the shelf worked identically whether a platform could support rich sidebar interaction or not, which is what made one design shippable across four different capability profiles.

What got in the way

Shipping in parallel with a live broadcast launch, inside an architecture I didn't author. The sequencing answer was to ship an MVP on one platform first, learn from it, then propagate — which is exactly the path the home-page revision took from Apple TV to Roku and Fire TV.

05

Solution

A live-first experience that keeps the broadcast running while a viewer browses — shipped across four platforms on the channel's launch date.

Key interaction. Picture-in-picture is the mechanism the whole design rests on. It lets live content stay visible during browsing, which means discovery never costs the viewer the thing they came for — the structural resolution of the live-versus-on-demand tension that had been driving drop-off.

Cross-platform system. One connected structure scaled per platform rather than to a lowest common denominator: the persistent live module and content shelf are constant, while interaction richness varies with what each surface can actually support.

Post-launch refinement. The home page moved from full-screen video to a collapsed video, restoring the viewer's sense of what else was navigable — shipped first on Apple TV, then carried into Roku and Fire TV.

What was delivered

  • Feature scope agreed with each Tech Platform Lead against real technical feasibility.

06

Impact and results

Shipped across four platforms on the channel's launch date, then refined three connected-TV platforms post-launch from real usage.

Business outcome. The product became the surface for a channel that went on to draw more than a million unique viewers in a single day within its first year. That figure belongs to the channel rather than to the design, and it's stated that way deliberately — the honest claim is that the experience didn't get in the way of that growth, not that it caused it.

User feedback. Early adopters drove the home-page revision, which is the clearest evidence the post-launch loop worked.

Measurement gap

  • The brief named a measurable problem — drop-off from content overload — and I can't report whether the shipped experience moved it. That's the single figure this case study needs, and the reason I now treat a baseline-and-readout agreement as part of scoping rather than as a post-launch nicety.

What worked

Taking the scope conversation to each Tech Platform Lead individually before committing to a design. Four platforms with different capabilities on a single launch date usually produces either a lowest-common-denominator product or a slipped date; talking feasibility through platform by platform meant each surface shipped at the level it could actually support. Choosing picture-in-picture was the other decision that held — it resolved the live-versus-discovery tension structurally rather than through navigation.

What I'd do differently

I'd run even a short round of user testing on the full-screen home-page concept before launch. The change that mattered most came from early adopters after shipping — that was the right correction, but it was a correction, and it was avoidable. I'd also insist the drop-off baseline be instrumented and re-read after launch as a condition of the engagement, since the brief named that measure and nobody closed the loop on it.

What I learned

When the problem is overload, the design work is negotiation, not addition — most of what I did on this project was decide what wouldn't be on screen, and then defend those absences to four platform teams and a client launching a broadcast channel at the same time. Shipping in parallel with a live launch also taught me how much of design leadership is sequencing: MVP first on one platform, learn, then propagate.
