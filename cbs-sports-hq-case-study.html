CBS Sports was launching CBS Sports HQ — a free, 24/7 streaming sports news channel — and needed a product experience to carry it across TV and mobile.

01

Situation

The existing CBS Sports surfing experience wasn't working: too much content, too many entry points, and a drop-off rate that pointed straight at cognitive overload.

The new channel needed a live-first experience that still let people find on-demand content, and it had to be built inside CBS Sports' existing architecture while the broadcast team was simultaneously testing and launching the channel itself.

Why it was hard

  • No clean slate. Everything had to be built within CBS Sports' existing architecture, so the design was constrained by decisions made long before the project started.
  • The existing experience was losing people. High drop-off from content overload meant the problem wasn't adding features — it was subtracting enough to make live viewing legible.
  • Four platforms, four sets of constraints, one launch date. TV, laptop, tablet, and phone had to ship as one connected structure, in parallel with the broadcast team's own launch, with each platform capable of a different amount.
02

Objective

Design a connected experience that made live broadcast the default without hiding on-demand content — and get it shipped across four platforms alongside a live channel launch.

My role: Led. Design lead across all four platforms, negotiating feature scope directly with each Tech Platform Lead.

Timeframe: Launched February 26, 2018.

Editor's note — add the engagement start date and total duration.

What success looked like

  • A live-first model that mimicked the ease of surfing broadcast TV, reducing the cognitive load that was driving drop-off.
  • One coherent experience across TV, laptop, tablet, and phone, scoped per platform rather than lowest-common-denominator.
  • Feature scope agreed with each Tech Platform Lead against real technical feasibility, not aspiration.
  • A shipped MVP, with a refinement path driven by real early-adopter behavior.
03

Actions

  1. 01

    Diagnosed the drop-off as a content-density problem, then designed subtraction. Reframed the brief from "surface more content" to "make live legible" — the structural decision that everything else followed from.

  2. 02

    Negotiated feature scope platform by platform with each Tech Platform Lead. Walked blue-sky concepts and wireframes through with every platform lead, scaling features down against each platform's real capabilities — which kept four surfaces on one launch date instead of one platform holding the rest hostage.

  3. 03

    Borrowed the live-news format to solve discoverability without sacrificing "always on." Studied how live TV news channels keep a broadcast running while surfacing other stories, and landed on picture-in-picture as the mechanism that let live content stay visible while a user browsed.

  4. 04

    Specified every state across the connected TV experience, for both new and returning users. Stitched together the full scenario set rather than the happy path — the work that made the experience shippable inside an existing architecture.

  5. 05

    Drove the post-launch revision that changed the home page. After the Apple TV MVP shipped, used early-adopter feedback to move the home page from full-screen video to a collapsed video — giving users a clearer sense of what else was navigable — and carried that refinement into Roku and Fire TV.

04

Impact

Shipped across four platforms on the channel's launch date, February 26, 2018 — CBS Sports HQ went live free and 24/7 across iOS and Android phones and tablets, Amazon Fire TV, Apple TV, and Roku via the CBS Sports app, plus the web.

Refined three connected-TV platforms post-launch from real usage — Apple TV's MVP shipped first, with Roku and Fire TV improved from early-adopter feedback.

What it delivered

  • Solved the stated problem: the home page revision reduced cognitive load by collapsing the full-screen video and clarifying what else users could navigate to.
  • The product became the surface for a channel that went on to grow substantially in its first year.

05

Reflection

What worked

Taking the scope conversation to each Tech Platform Lead individually before committing to a design. Four platforms with different capabilities on a single launch date usually produces either a lowest-common-denominator product or a slipped date; talking feasibility through platform by platform meant each surface shipped at the level it could actually support. Choosing picture-in-picture was the other decision that held — it resolved the live-versus-discovery tension structurally rather than through navigation, which is why the experience stayed simple enough to address the overload problem.

What I'd refine

We validated against platform leads and stakeholders, not users, before launch — and the change that mattered most, collapsing the home page video, came from early adopters after shipping. That was the right correction, but it was a correction. Even a short round of testing on the full-screen concept would have surfaced the control problem before it reached customers. I'd also want the drop-off baseline instrumented from the start: the brief named a measurable problem, and I'd insist the same measure be read after launch.

What I learned

When the problem is overload, the design work is negotiation, not addition — most of what I did on this project was decide what wouldn't be on screen, and then defend those absences to four platform teams and a client launching a broadcast channel at the same time. Shipping in parallel with a live launch also taught me how much of design leadership is sequencing: MVP first on one platform, learn, then propagate.
