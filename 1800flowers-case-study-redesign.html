01

Project overview

Design lead on the engagement, owning conversational design, flow architecture, and the research protocol.

Because there was nothing visual to hand off, my output was the engineering specification — which meant the responsibility ran further down the build than it normally would. Every branch that wasn't resolved in the flow was a branch nobody would resolve at all.

Team, tools & scope

  • Team. Accedo strategy and the client's product team; direct working sessions with the Amazon Pay team.
  • Tools.
  • Methods. Partner discovery calls, conversational flow architecture, Wizard-of-Oz utterance testing, affinity mapping, emotional and tonal session coding.
  • Scope. 4 core scenarios architected to command level · ~30 flow nodes · 8 test participants · one living utterance library handed to the client.
02

Problem statement

Take someone from "order flowers for my mom's birthday" to a completed, paid order — using only speech.

Business context. Voice commerce was a genuinely open category at the time. Completing a purchase inside a skill, rather than handing off to a phone, was the differentiator the client was after, and Amazon Pay's voice-purchase path was the mechanism for it.

Why it mattered. Gifting is time-pressured and often done badly under that pressure. A voice flow that could recall a previous order and re-send it turned the platform's constraint — no browsing — into an advantage, provided the conversation could carry the whole transaction.

Why it had stalled

  • The critical flow wasn't documented. Amazon Pay's voice-purchase path didn't appear in the public Alexa developer guide, so the constraints governing the entire checkout were unknown before the project started.
  • No screen meant no fallback. Every edge case — a mishearing, a wrong address, an abandoned order — had to be resolved in language, since there was no layout to absorb ambiguity.
  • Design output had to serve as engineering spec. With nothing visual to hand off, the flows themselves were the build documentation, which raised the bar on precision far past normal design fidelity.

User pain points

Testing surfaced three that shaped the design directly. People didn't know order history was accessible by voice, and reached for it only when prompted — "send the same as last time" was a phrase participants produced spontaneously once they realised it was possible. People asked for price before size, inverting the order the flow originally assumed. And cancellation produced audible hesitation: with no screen to confirm against, participants wanted a confirmation code read back to them before they'd trust that anything had happened.

03

Research and discovery

Two discovery problems at once: an undocumented partner API, and a product that didn't exist yet to test.

Partner discovery. The Amazon Pay constraints weren't in any guide, so I established them through a series of direct calls with the Amazon Pay team, iterating the user flow with them and the client as each constraint surfaced.

Utterance testing — 8 participants. Rather than wait for a build, I simulated the device: laptops either side of a dimmed screen, a facilitator voicing Alexa live over Zoom, and an unplugged physical Alexa unit in front of the participant so the interaction stayed physically realistic. Every deviation in phrasing was logged.

Analysis. Sessions were coded by participant background, emotional state, and tone of voice alongside the functional tasks — so patterns in language could be traced back to the moment and mindset that produced them. The reorder and cancellation insights both came out of that coding rather than out of task-completion data.

Competitive context.

Key findings

  • People reorder by memory, not by catalogue. "Send the same as last time" only appeared when someone was actually speaking to a device — it never came up in scripted review.
  • Confirmation is the substitute for a screen. Hesitation in the cancellation flow indicated participants needed a spoken confirmation code to believe the action had registered.
  • Voice can't describe a bouquet. Participants without floristry knowledge couldn't picture species and colours from description alone — the one problem the medium structurally cannot solve.

04

Design process

  1. 01

    Reverse-engineered an undocumented payment flow through direct access. Established what the voice-purchase flow could and couldn't do by going to the source, then iterated the user flow as each constraint surfaced — rather than designing against assumptions and discovering the limits in build.

  2. 02

    Architected four core scenarios down to the command level. Mapped new user, returning user, browsing, and purchase — including what Alexa should volunteer versus wait to be asked, reorder logic, occasion-based recommendation, and the Amazon Pay handoff with address and payment fallbacks — branching to roughly 30 nodes.

  3. 03

    Invented a testing method for a product that didn't exist yet. The unplugged Alexa unit was the detail that mattered: participants addressed the object rather than the facilitator, which is what kept their speech patterns natural instead of conversational.

  4. 04

    Turned the research artifact into infrastructure the client owned. Structured the utterance library so the 1-800-Flowers team could keep adding to it after the engagement ended, rather than handing over a findings report with a shelf life.

  5. 05

    Coded sessions by emotion and tone, not just task. Sorting by emotional state alongside functional success is where the reorder and cancellation insights came from — neither would have surfaced in a completion-rate view.

Iterations from testing

Testing changed the flow in three specific places: order history was surfaced proactively rather than waiting to be asked for, price moved ahead of size in the recommendation sequence, and the cancellation path gained a spoken confirmation code. Each change traces to a logged utterance rather than to a design opinion.

What got in the way

Designing without a screen exposed how much visual interfaces let you defer decisions. A layout can hold ambiguity; a sentence can't. Every branch had to be resolved explicitly in language, which made the work slower and the specification far more rigorous than an equivalent screen-based project.

05

Solution

A skill that carries a gift purchase from intent to paid order without a screen, specified precisely enough that engineers built from the flows alone.

Key interactions. Occasion-based recommendation opens the conversation; reorder is offered rather than requested, because testing showed people didn't know to ask; arrangement and size are confirmed in sequence with price stated first; and the Amazon Pay handoff completes the transaction in-skill, with address and payment fallbacks handled in language.

The design system, for voice. With no components to build, the system was linguistic: a defined persona and register for Alexa's responses, consistent confirmation and error patterns, rules for what the assistant volunteers versus waits to be asked, and the utterance library mapping how real people phrase each intent. That library is the closest analogue to a component set this project had, and it was the artifact the client kept.

Reviewing without visuals. The transcript format became the team's wireframe. Reading exchanges aloud in review was how flows were evaluated internally — the only reliable way to catch a sentence that scanned well but didn't speak well.

What was delivered

  • Flows covering new user, returning user, browsing, and purchase, specified to individual spoken commands.
  • A working understanding of Amazon Pay's voice-purchase constraints, established through direct access rather than documentation.
  • An utterance library grounded in how real people speak, structured for the client to extend.
  • Documentation that functioned as engineering spec, removing the translation layer between design and build.

06

Impact and results

The skill shipped as one of the earliest to complete a purchase end to end through Amazon Pay, at a point when that path was undocumented and largely unproven.

Business outcome. The engagement established the Amazon Pay voice-purchase constraints for the client's product team — knowledge that didn't exist in public documentation at the time — and left them an utterance library they continued to extend. The research output became a durable asset rather than a one-off report, which is the outcome I'd argue mattered most: the client could keep improving the skill without us.

User feedback.

Measurement gap

  • The design and research outcomes are well evidenced; the commercial one isn't. No completion rate, install count, or voice-order volume was instrumented in a form I can point to — which on a first-of-its-kind build was a loss for the client as much as for the case study.

What worked

Building the test rig before the product existed. Putting an unplugged Alexa in front of people and voicing it live got us real speech patterns months earlier than waiting for a build would have — and phrasings like "send the same as last time" only surface when someone is actually talking to a device, not reading a script. Coding sessions by tone and emotional state was the other decision that paid off.

What I'd do differently

I'd run a second, larger round of utterance testing before launch rather than treating eight participants as sufficient — in voice, the failures live in the long tail, and the first round is designed to find the head. I'd also fix the measurement question at the start of the engagement rather than the end. The work produced excellent documentation and a real shipped product, and I still can't point to what it did commercially.

What I learned

Designing without a screen exposed how much visual interfaces let you defer decisions. A layout can hold ambiguity; a sentence can't. That permanently changed how I think about edge cases in any medium.

What I'd build next

An Alexa Show version. Testing showed that people without floristry knowledge couldn't picture species and colours from description alone — the one problem voice structurally cannot solve, and the obvious next move for the product.
