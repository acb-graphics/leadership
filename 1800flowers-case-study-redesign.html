1-800-Flowers wanted customers to be able to find and send a gift by talking — no app, no screen, no browsing.

01

Situation

That meant building one of the first Alexa skills to complete a purchase through Amazon Pay, at a point when almost nobody had done it.

The company had a clear commercial goal and no map for how to get there.

Why it had stalled

  • The critical flow wasn't documented. Amazon Pay's voice-purchase path didn't appear in the public Alexa developer guide, so the constraints governing the entire checkout were unknown before the project started.
  • No screen meant no fallback. Every edge case — a mishearing, a wrong address, an abandoned order — had to be resolved in language, since there was no layout to absorb ambiguity.
  • Design output had to serve as engineering spec. With nothing visual to hand off, the flows themselves were the build documentation, which raised the bar on precision far past normal design fidelity.
02

Objective

Deliver a voice experience that takes someone from "order flowers for my mom's birthday" to a completed, paid order — and produce documentation precise enough for engineers to build from without a single mockup.

My role: Led. Lead Product Designer on the engagement — I owned the conversational design, the flow architecture, and the research protocol, working alongside Accedo strategy and the client's product team.

Timeframe: To be confirmed.

What success looked like

  • A working understanding of Amazon Pay's voice-purchase constraints, established through direct access rather than documentation.
  • Flows covering new user, returning user, browsing, and purchase — each specified to the level of individual spoken commands.
  • An utterance library grounded in how real people speak, not how the design team assumed they would.
  • A handoff package the client's own team could extend and keep testing after the engagement ended.
03

Actions

  1. 01

    Reverse-engineered an undocumented payment flow through direct access. Ran a series of calls with the Amazon Pay team to establish what the voice-purchase flow could and couldn't do, then iterated the user flow with both them and the client as each constraint surfaced.

  2. 02

    Architected four core scenarios down to the command level. Mapped new user, returning user, browsing, and purchase — including what Alexa should volunteer versus wait to be asked, reorder logic, occasion-based recommendation, and the Amazon Pay handoff with address and payment fallbacks — branching to roughly 30 nodes.

  3. 03

    Invented a testing method for a product that didn't exist yet. Simulated the device: laptops either side of a dimmed screen, a facilitator voicing Alexa live over Zoom, and an unplugged physical Alexa unit in front of the participant so the interaction stayed physically realistic despite there being no software to test.

  4. 04

    Built a living utterance library from eight sessions and handed it off as an asset. Logged every deviation in phrasing into an utterance sheet, then structured it so the 1-800-Flowers team could keep adding to it after the engagement — turning a research artifact into infrastructure the client owned.

  5. 05

    Coded sessions by emotion and tone, not just task. Sorted every session by participant background, emotional state, and tone of voice alongside the functional tasks, so patterns in language could be traced back to the moment and mindset that produced them — which is where the reorder and cancellation insights came from.

05

Reflection

What worked

Building the test rig before the product existed. Putting an unplugged Alexa in front of people and voicing it live got us real speech patterns months earlier than waiting for a build would have — and phrasings like "send the same as last time" only surface when someone is actually talking to a device, not reading a script. Coding sessions by tone and emotional state was the other decision that paid off: hesitation in the cancellation flow was what told us people wanted a confirmation code read back, and that never shows up in task-completion data.

What I'd refine

Eight participants was enough to find the obvious phrasings, not enough to be confident about the long tail — and in voice, the long tail is where the failures live. I'd also push earlier for agreement on how success would be measured post-launch. The engagement produced excellent design documentation and a real shipped product, but the business outcome isn't instrumented in a way I can point to, and on a first-of-its-kind build that evidence would have been valuable to the client too.

What I learned

Designing without a screen exposed how much visual interfaces let you defer decisions. A layout can hold ambiguity; a sentence can't. Every branch had to be resolved explicitly in language, which made the work slower and the specification far more rigorous than an equivalent screen-based project — and permanently changed how I think about edge cases in any medium.

What I'd build next

An Alexa Show version. Testing showed that people without floristry knowledge couldn't picture species and colors from description alone — the one problem voice structurally cannot solve.
