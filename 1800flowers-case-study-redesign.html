1-800-Flowers offers same-day delivery of quality flowers and gifts from local florists. We were brought on for strategy, tech discovery and design of their first voice-ordering app — built to help users find and send the perfect arrangement for a specific occasion, entirely by talking.

01

Challenge

This was one of the first Alexa apps to integrate with Amazon Pay — a net-new, largely undocumented feature at the time.

We scheduled multiple calls with the Amazon Pay team to understand the payment flow, since none of it appeared in the public Alexa developer guide. The user flow went through several rounds of iteration with both the client and the Amazon Pay team as we mapped out its limitations.

Working with no visuals, we presented flows across a range of scenarios that doubled as engineering documentation.

Why it was hard

  • Amazon Pay's voice-purchase flow wasn't publicly documented — we reverse-engineered it through direct calls with their team.
  • No screen meant every edge case had to be resolved in language, not layout.
  • Flows had to double as literal engineering specs, not just design references.
02

Strategy

Designing for voice starts with a happy path: a wake word, a launch word, an invocation name, and an utterance that completes a command.

We whiteboarded a base flow for a new user, a returning user, a browsing experience and a purchase flow — then built out the specific commands inside each scenario for the dev team to build against. Because these were our own interpretation of how people would actually speak, we needed early user testing to surface the other words and phrasings real users would reach for.

Core scenarios mapped

  • New user — first launch, onboarding, and what Alexa should volunteer vs. wait to be asked.
  • Returning user — reorder logic and order-status lookups.
  • Browsing — recommending by occasion or recipient when the user doesn't know what they want yet.
  • Purchase — the Amazon Pay handoff, confirmation, and address/payment fallback.

03

Discovery

Since the skill wasn't built yet, we simulated the device itself.

We set up laptops on both sides of a dimmed screen, dialed into a Zoom call so a facilitator could speak as Alexa, and placed an unplugged Alexa device in front of the interviewee to mimic how someone actually interacts with the hardware. We interviewed 8 people in the office to build an utterance sheet of alternate phrasings, which we handed to the 1-800-Flowers team for further additions and testing.

Testing setup

  • 8 in-office participants, one facilitator voicing Alexa live over Zoom.
  • An unplugged physical Alexa device kept the interaction realistic despite no working software.
  • Every deviation in phrasing was logged into a growing utterance sheet.

04

Approach

With no visuals to design, the approach was to make the documentation itself extremely thorough.

The user flow was revised repeatedly as testing surfaced new requirements, and every session's notes were sorted by who the participant was, their emotional state, and their tone of voice, alongside how they launched, placed, reordered, and cancelled — so patterns in language could be traced back to the moment that produced them.

Signal we tracked per session

  • Who — participant background and familiarity with voice assistants.
  • Tone of voice & emotional state — hesitant vs. confident phrasing.
  • Task-specific language — launching, placing, reordering, cancelling.

05

Outcome

The result was an Alexa skill that recommends a gift for a specific occasion and completes ordering with a handful of spoken commands — one of the first voice experiences to close the loop with Amazon Pay end-to-end.
