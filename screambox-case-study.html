01

Project overview

Designer on an OTT app built for an unreleased console, working closely with engineering to establish what was even buildable.

Deliverables were UX design, wireframes, prototyping and design QA. The defining condition was the absence of a specification: with no native standards to follow, every proposal was a hypothesis that engineering had to confirm before it could become a design.

Team, tools & scope

  • Team. Accedo design and engineering, with the Screambox client team.
  • Tools.
  • Methods. Blue-sky wireframing under zero documentation, engineering feasibility review, pattern language development from scratch, controller-navigation prototyping, walkthrough video for client demo, design QA.
  • Scope. 0 existing design guidelines to start from · 1 console launch to ship against · full browse, detail and playback experience for controller input.
02

Problem statement

Launch a horror streaming app alongside a console that didn't exist yet, with no written development or design constraints to build against.

Business context. A console launch is a rare acquisition window: early adopters arrive looking for things to put on a new device, and the apps present on day one get disproportionate attention. Missing the window meant competing for the same audience later, without the momentum.

Why it mattered. For a niche service, being one of the few apps available at launch is worth more than being a better app six months in. The deadline wasn't negotiable and the specification didn't exist — which made feasibility, not craft, the binding constraint.

Why it was hard

  • No documentation existed. PS4 was unreleased, so there were no written development or design constraints to work from.
  • No native navigation or controller standards. Feasibility had to be established directly with engineering, proposal by proposal.
  • Familiar and new at once. The app had to feel instantly recognisable to anyone who had used an OTT service, while fitting a control scheme nobody had used yet.

User pain points

Horror is a browsing-led genre: viewers arrive to see what's there rather than to find a specific title, which puts unusual weight on a large library being scannable. The console context adds a second problem — a controller is a poor instrument for text entry, so any design that leaned on search rather than browse would have failed regardless of how good the search was.

These were reasoned from the genre and the platform rather than established with viewers. No user research or usability testing was run on this engagement — see below.

03

Research and discovery

No user research was run. Discovery was entirely technical and referential — establishing feasibility with engineering, and borrowing conventions from platforms viewers already knew.

Feasibility discovery. With no PS4 documentation, engineering was the only source of truth about what the hardware and its input model would support. Wireframes functioned as questions: each one was reviewed for buildability before it advanced, which is a slower loop than designing to a spec but the only one available.

Referential discovery. Navigation was modelled on the PS4 home experience itself, on the reasoning that whatever conventions Sony established would be the ones users learned first. Content organisation borrowed the row-based structure common to OTT services, so a large library stayed scannable without inventing a new browsing paradigm at the same time as everything else.

Competitive analysis.

What discovery established

  • The console's own interface was the safest reference. Modelling navigation on the PS4 home experience meant the app inherited whatever mental model users formed from the device itself.
  • Rows solve library scale. Organised rows let an extensive catalogue stay browsable on a controller, where a grid or a search-first model would not.
  • Focus state is the whole interaction. With controller input there's no cursor and no touch — the only thing telling a user where they are is the focus treatment, which made it the highest-priority visual decision rather than a detail.

04

Design process

  1. 01

    Wireframed blue-sky, then let engineering set the boundary. With no written constraints, the only way to find the edges of the platform was to propose past them and iterate back — treating each wireframe as a feasibility question rather than a specification.

  2. 02

    Modelled navigation on the PS4 home experience itself. Chose the console's own interface as the reference point, on the reasoning that it would be the pattern every user learned first — inheriting familiarity in a context where none could be assumed.

  3. 03

    Built a pattern language from scratch for controller navigation. Defined focus states, row behaviour, and the detail-overlay pattern from nothing, since no native component set existed to extend.

  4. 04

    Reduced the content detail view to two actions. Add to Queue and Trailer as the only primary actions, on the reasoning that a controller makes every additional choice expensive — one press to save, one press to sample.

  5. 05

    Produced a walkthrough video to demonstrate navigation and transitions. Motion and focus behaviour can't be reviewed in static screens, so the walkthrough became the artifact the client actually signed off on, and the reference for design QA during build.

Decisions and rationale

The decision that shaped the visual work most was reserving red for focus and calls to action only. A horror brand invites red everywhere, and on a controller-driven interface that would have been actively harmful — if everything is red, nothing indicates where you are. Constraining the accent to a functional role let the brand stay atmospheric through backgrounds and texture while the one saturated colour did navigational work.

Testing and iteration

Iteration ran against engineering feasibility and client review rather than user testing. Design QA during build was the final loop — checking that focus behaviour and transitions survived implementation on hardware that had only just become available.

05

Solution

A cinematic, browse-led horror app with a pattern language built from scratch for controller navigation.

Key features and interactions. A cinematic landing experience sets tone immediately, with organised content rows beneath it that make an extensive library scannable without text entry. The content detail overlay carries exactly two primary actions — Add to Queue and Trailer — so the most common decisions cost one press each. Transitions and controls are native to the PlayStation experience rather than ported from mobile or web patterns.

Design system elements. With no native component set to extend, the pattern language was built from nothing: row and card behaviour, the focus state that indicates position under controller input, the detail-overlay structure, and the transition model between browse and playback. That system is the durable output of the project — it's what made an app of this scale designable and buildable in the time available.

Visual design choices. Cinematic backgrounds and haunting textures carry the brand without competing with content thumbnails, which have to stay legible for browsing to work. Red is the single accent, reserved for focus states and calls to action so it reads as navigation rather than decoration. The overall treatment is dark and low-contrast by design, with the accent and the focus ring doing the wayfinding.

Demo. A walkthrough video demonstrating controller navigation and transitions, used for client sign-off and as the reference during design QA.

What was delivered

  • A complete browse, detail and playback experience designed for controller input.
  • A pattern language built from scratch, with no native standards to extend.
  • A walkthrough demonstrating navigation and motion, used for sign-off and QA.
  • An app shipped in conjunction with the console launch.

06

Impact and results

The app shipped in conjunction with the PS4 launch, giving a niche service a foothold with the console's earliest adopters ahead of competing OTT apps on the platform.

Business outcome. The strategic value was timing. Presence in the launch window is an acquisition advantage a small service can't buy later, and hitting it with a complete, controller-native experience rather than a compromised port is what the engagement delivered.

User feedback.

Measurement gap

  • No download, engagement or retention figure was shared or instrumented in a form I can point to. The launch-window argument is sound but unproven, and this is the case study on the site with the least outcome evidence behind it.

What worked

Treating the console's own interface as the design reference. With no documentation and no precedent, borrowing the mental model users would form from the hardware itself was the lowest-risk way to make an unfamiliar control scheme feel familiar. Reserving red for focus and calls to action was the other decision that held — it let a horror brand stay atmospheric without compromising the one signal a controller-driven interface depends on.

What I'd do differently

I'd get the app in front of horror viewers, even briefly. Nothing in this process tested whether the browse-led structure actually helped people find something to watch — the design reasoning is sound but it's reasoning, not evidence. I'd also negotiate access to launch-window numbers as part of the engagement. Shipping into a console launch is a claim about timing, and without a download figure it stays a claim.

What I learned

When there's no specification, engineering isn't a constraint on design — it's the research method. The wireframes on this project were questions rather than answers, and getting comfortable proposing things that might be impossible, then iterating back to what was buildable, is a working mode I've used on every emerging-platform project since.
