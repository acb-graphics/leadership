Case Study — Cross-Functional Research & Experience Strategy

Deciem

Aligning five disconnected departments around one shared picture of the customer.

Client
Deciem | The Abnormal Beauty Company (The Ordinary, NIOD, Hylamide)
Role
Senior Experience Consultant & Researcher
Started
2019
Duration
Length to be confirmed

Deciem was fluent in its own product strategy — ingredients, pricing philosophy, growth plan across a portfolio of brands. What it didn't have was any clear read on what customers thought, brand by brand or about the umbrella as a whole.

01

Situation

Customer service heard the frustration daily. Marketing, design, and branding never did.

Each team was solving a real problem, but a different one, and none of them were connected to the product roadmap.

Why it had stalled

  • Strategy without customer evidence. Internal conviction about product-market growth wasn't matched by any structured view of customer perspective per brand.
  • Five teams, five versions of the truth. Service, floor sales, marketing, design, and branding each held a different perception of the brand — and the teams furthest from customers had the most influence over the experience.
  • No research infrastructure to build on. The alignment problem and the research capability had to be solved simultaneously; there was no existing practice to plug into.
02

Objective

Give Deciem a single, evidence-backed view of customer need — and make it credible enough that five departments would actually prioritize against it together.

My role: Led. I was the sole researcher and experience strategist on the engagement, working across departments without formal authority over any of them — influence was the operating mode throughout.

Timeframe: Engaged from 2019.

Editor's note — add total engagement length once confirmed.

What success looked like

  • A shared, cross-departmental understanding of positioning and customer need, replacing five competing versions.
  • A prioritization roadmap separating urgent quick wins from larger scope-of-work items.
  • Primary research on the digital experience, targeted at where customers were actually dropping off.
  • A credible answer to product education and sustainability — the brand's two live strategic questions — beyond physical retail.
03

Actions

  1. 01

    Interviewed all five departments individually to surface the real disagreement. Ran 1:1 sessions with customer service, floor sales, marketing, design, and branding — which exposed that the teams shaping the brand had no exposure to what customer service was hearing every day.

  2. 02

    Built a prioritization framework and ran card sorting to force cross-team consensus. Turned each customer pain point into a card and had teams sort them live against brand-perception impact, converting five separate agendas into one ranked list nobody could dismiss as an outside opinion.

  3. 03

    Used analytics to decide what to test, rather than guessing. Pulled Google Analytics drop-off data to identify the highest-friction screens and wrote them directly into the usability-testing script — so qualitative sessions were aimed at proven problem areas.

  4. 04

    Designed and ran a paired research protocol with customers aged 24–64. Structured each Toronto session as 30 minutes of Q&A on shopping behavior and sustainability plus 30 minutes of usability testing, gathering strategic and tactical evidence in one sitting.

  5. 05

    Prototyped two Spark AR concepts to solve product education where customers already were. Chose Spark AR because Deciem's customers live on Instagram and Facebook, then built Virtual Foundation Matching with the customer happiness team and lab assistants calibrating against real skin tones, and a Terracycle filter with the sustainability team that identifies a bottle and routes it to the correct recycling stream.

05

Reflection

What worked

Making the research undeniable rather than persuasive. The card-sorting sessions meant teams built the priority list themselves, so nobody had to be sold on it afterward — and presenting unfiltered customer interviews rather than a summarized findings deck removed the room's ability to argue with the interpretation. Anchoring the usability script in analytics drop-off data was the other decision that paid off: it meant every session was spent on a screen already known to be failing.

What I'd refine

All participants were Toronto-based, for a portfolio sold internationally — the regional read was solid, the global one wasn't. I'd also push harder, earlier, for agreement on how the quick wins would be measured after they shipped. The engagement produced strong alignment and a clear roadmap, but I'd want the follow-through instrumented so the value of the work is visible in the business's own numbers rather than in a report.

What I learned

The gap in most organizations isn't a lack of customer insight — it's that the insight sits with the teams who have the least power to act on it. Customer service knew the problems. My job wasn't to discover them so much as to give them a route into the rooms where roadmaps get set, in a form that couldn't be filtered on the way.
