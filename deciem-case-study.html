Work / Deciem
Case Study — Cross-Functional Research & Experience Strategy

Deciem

Five teams held five versions of the customer. The ones furthest from customers had the most influence over the experience.

Client
Deciem | The Abnormal Beauty Company (The Ordinary, NIOD, Hylamide)
Role
Started
2019
Duration

Deciem was fluent in its own product strategy — ingredients, pricing philosophy, growth plan across a portfolio of brands. What it didn't have was any clear read on what customers thought, brand by brand or about the umbrella as a whole.

01

Project overview

Sole researcher and experience strategist, working across five departments without formal authority over any of them.

Influence was the operating mode throughout. I owned the research design, ran every session, built the prioritisation framework, and presented findings into rooms where roadmaps were set — but I couldn't direct any of the teams whose work the findings implicated, which shaped every methodological choice on the project.

Team, tools & scope

  • Team.
  • Tools. Google Analytics for drop-off diagnosis;
  • Methods. 1:1 departmental interviews, card sorting, analytics-led usability scripting, paired qualitative sessions (Q&A + usability), AR concept prototyping.
  • Scope. 5 departments · 3 brands under one umbrella · 2 Spark AR concepts · participants aged 24–64, Toronto-based.
02

Problem statement

Customer service heard the frustration daily. Marketing, design, and branding never did.

Business context. Deciem was growing fast across a portfolio of brands with distinct identities, and had two live strategic questions — product education and sustainability — that it wanted answered beyond physical retail. Each team was solving a real problem, but a different one, and none of them were connected to the product roadmap.

Why it mattered. This was an organisational problem wearing a design problem's clothes. Any redesign would have been overwritten within a quarter unless the five teams first agreed on what was actually wrong — so the work had to produce alignment, not just recommendations.

Why it had stalled

  • Strategy without customer evidence. Internal conviction about product-market growth wasn't matched by any structured view of customer perspective per brand.
  • Five teams, five versions of the truth. Service, floor sales, marketing, design, and branding each held a different perception of the brand — and the teams furthest from customers had the most influence over the experience.
  • No research infrastructure to build on. The alignment problem and the research capability had to be solved simultaneously; there was no existing practice to plug into.

User pain points

The customer-facing pain points were already known inside the business — customer service had been logging them for years. The problem wasn't discovery, it was routing: the people who held the evidence had the least influence over the roadmap. Analytics confirmed where the digital experience was losing people, and the regimen pages were a specific failure point for anyone unfamiliar with active ingredients, who couldn't tell why a combination had been recommended or what each product actually did.

03

Research and discovery

Two research tracks running at once: one internal, to surface where the five departments actually disagreed, and one external, aimed at the screens analytics had already flagged as failing.

Internal. 1:1 sessions with customer service, floor sales, marketing, design, and branding, run separately so each team's account wasn't moderated by the presence of the others.

External. Each Toronto session was structured as 30 minutes of Q&A on shopping behaviour and sustainability plus 30 minutes of usability testing — gathering strategic and tactical evidence in one sitting. Participants ranged from 24 to 64.

Participants: internal interviews · customer sessions

Data and evidence. Google Analytics drop-off data identified the highest-friction screens, and those screens were written directly into the usability script — so every qualitative session was spent on a surface already known to be failing rather than on a guess.

Key findings

  • The influence gap was the finding. The internal interviews exposed that the teams shaping the brand had no exposure to what customer service was hearing every day — which reframed the engagement from a digital-experience project to an alignment one.
  • Regimen pages failed the non-expert. Customers unfamiliar with active ingredients couldn't answer why a combination was recommended, what the ingredients did, or how the routine addressed their concern.

04

Design process

  1. 01

    Interviewed all five departments individually to surface the real disagreement. Running the sessions separately was deliberate: in a joint session the teams with the most authority would have set the terms, and the account I needed was the one from the teams with the least.

  2. 02

    Built a prioritisation framework and ran card sorting to force cross-team consensus. Turned each customer pain point into a card and had teams sort them live against brand-perception impact — converting five separate agendas into one ranked list nobody could dismiss as an outside opinion, because they had built it themselves.

  3. 03

    Used analytics to decide what to test, rather than guessing. Pulled drop-off data to identify the highest-friction screens and wrote them into the usability script, so limited qualitative time was spent only on proven problem areas.

  4. 04

    Tested three regimen-page variants against the questions a non-expert actually has. Each variant phrased the recommendation differently — one leading with skin type and concern, one explaining what each ingredient targets, one leading with the routine itself — to find which framing let someone unfamiliar with actives understand why they'd been given that combination.

  5. 05

    Prototyped two Spark AR concepts to solve product education where customers already were. Chose Spark AR because Deciem's customers live on Instagram and Facebook, then built Virtual Foundation Matching with the customer happiness team and lab assistants calibrating against real skin tones, and a Terracycle filter with the sustainability team that identifies a bottle and routes it to the correct recycling stream.

Decisions and rationale

The decision that shaped the outcome most was presenting unfiltered interview footage rather than a summarised findings deck. A summary invites interpretation, and interpretation is what lets a room argue with research it doesn't like. Footage removed that move entirely — the teams that had never spoken to a customer watched one, and the discussion started from what was said rather than from whether I'd read it correctly.

What got in the way

No formal authority over any of the five departments, and no existing research practice to plug into. Both were resolved structurally rather than politically: card sorting made the teams the authors of the priority list, and analytics made the testing agenda a matter of record rather than opinion.

05

Solution

A two-tier prioritisation roadmap that split immediate fixes from larger scope-of-work items, plus two Spark AR concepts aimed at the brand's two live strategic questions.

Key features. Virtual Foundation Matching addressed product education by letting someone see a shade on their own face — built with the customer happiness team and lab assistants calibrating against real skin tones, so the matching logic came from people who handled the products daily rather than from a colour library. The Terracycle filter addressed sustainability by identifying a bottle through the camera and routing it to the correct recycling stream, turning an abstract commitment into a single action a customer could take at the bin.

Why Spark AR. The platform choice was the strategy. Deciem's customers already lived on Instagram and Facebook, so meeting them there removed the acquisition step that a standalone app or a new site section would have required.

Design system elements.

What was delivered

  • A shared, cross-departmental understanding of positioning and customer need, replacing five competing versions.
  • A prioritisation roadmap separating urgent quick wins from larger scope-of-work items.
  • Primary research on the digital experience, targeted at where customers were actually dropping off.
  • Two AR concepts answering product education and sustainability beyond physical retail.

06

Impact and results

Five departments aligned on one prioritised view of customer need, and a two-tier roadmap that gave leadership a sequencing decision instead of an undifferentiated backlog.

Business outcome. The durable change was structural: customer service's evidence acquired a route into the rooms where roadmaps get set, in a form that couldn't be filtered on the way. Two Spark AR concepts extended the brand's education and sustainability positioning beyond physical retail.

Did the concepts ship?

User feedback.

Measurement gap

  • The alignment outcome is real but qualitative. What's missing is the follow-through: how many quick wins shipped, and whether drop-off, conversion, or support-ticket volume moved as a result. Those figures exist in Deciem's own systems and are the ones this case study most needs.

What worked

Making the research undeniable rather than persuasive. The card-sorting sessions meant teams built the priority list themselves, so nobody had to be sold on it afterward — and presenting unfiltered customer interviews rather than a summarised findings deck removed the room's ability to argue with the interpretation. Anchoring the usability script in analytics drop-off data was the other decision that paid off: it meant every session was spent on a screen already known to be failing.

What I'd do differently

I'd negotiate the measurement plan into the engagement before the research started, rather than hoping it followed. The work produced strong alignment and a clear roadmap, but the value of it lives in a report instead of in the business's own numbers — and a roadmap nobody instruments is a roadmap that quietly stops being credited. I'd also widen the sample beyond Toronto for a portfolio sold internationally, even if that meant fewer, shorter sessions across more markets.

What I learned

The gap in most organisations isn't a lack of customer insight — it's that the insight sits with the teams who have the least power to act on it. Customer service knew the problems. My job wasn't to discover them so much as to give them a route into the rooms where roadmaps get set, in a form that couldn't be filtered on the way.
