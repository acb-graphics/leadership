01

Project overview

A proof-of-concept engagement: flow architecture, in-vehicle interface design, and an interactive prototype, working with Honda's in-house design team and Parkopedia's development team.

This is concept work and the page treats it that way throughout. Nothing here shipped to drivers under this engagement, and the outcome I'd defend is the reframing — the work turned a partial happy-path concept into a complete definition of the problem, which is a different and more useful contribution than a demo.

Team, tools & scope

  • Team. Honda's in-house design team, Parkopedia's development team, and Honda R&D stakeholders.
  • Tools. InVision for the interactive prototype;
  • Methods. Audit of existing in-house designs, gap analysis, driving-state modelling, technical discovery with engineering, interactive prototyping for prioritisation.
  • Scope. 3 driving states modelled — in situ, in motion, parked · 2 platforms' payment-security models resolved — CarPlay and Android Auto · error and edge states designed across both lanes.
02

Problem statement

Honda's in-house team had already produced a set of designs — but they covered the version of the problem where everything goes right. In a car, almost nothing about the problem is that simple.

Business context. Honda R&D Innovations was evaluating whether in-vehicle parking payment was a viable product direction and whether Parkopedia was the right partner for it. The concept had to be good enough to answer both questions as a real product decision rather than a demo.

Why it mattered. A payment transaction inside a moving vehicle is a safety question before it's a UX question. Shipping the happy path alone would have meant shipping an interface with no answer at exactly the moments that carry risk.

Why it had stalled

  • Only the happy path existed. Error states weren't designed, which in an in-vehicle context means the interface has no answer at exactly the moments it matters.
  • The in-motion state hadn't been considered at all. Searching and paying while the car is moving is a fundamentally different design problem from doing it parked — and it was simply absent from the existing work.
  • Payment security was an unknown across two platforms. CarPlay and Android Auto handle stored payment methods differently, and nobody had established what either would permit before designing against it.

User pain points

The driver problem is that parking decisions arrive under time pressure at exactly the moment attention is least available — approaching a destination, in traffic, often unfamiliar with the area. The interface has to resolve price, availability and distance in a glance, or it competes with the road.

These pain points were reasoned from the driving context rather than established with drivers. No driver research was run on this engagement — see Research and discovery below.

03

Research and discovery

No user research was run. Discovery here was a design audit and a technical investigation — and the case study is weaker for the first absence, which the reflection addresses directly.

Design audit. I analysed Honda's internal design team's existing work and mapped it against the full set of user states, which is how the two gaps surfaced: error states weren't designed, and the in-motion experience was entirely absent. That audit is what reframed the engagement from execution to definition.

Technical discovery. Working through the development team, I established how CarPlay and Android Auto each handle stored payment methods. This was the project's largest unknown, and resolving it before design rather than during it is what kept the payment flow from being invalidated late.

Driver research: none. The concept was reviewed with stakeholders and the development team. That is design review, not user validation, and for an interface intended for use at speed it's the gap I'd close first.

Competitive analysis.

Key findings

  • Driving state, not user type, is the organising variable. The same task splits into three different products depending on whether the driver is approaching, moving, or stopped — which became the structural spine of the concept.
  • Platform security constraints determine what's designable. CarPlay and Android Auto differ in how they permit stored payment methods, so the payment flow had to be designed to the stricter of the two rather than to an idealised model.
  • The happy path was the smallest part of the problem. Most of the real experience lives in the states the original designs didn't cover.
04

Design process

  1. 01

    Audited the existing in-house designs and named what wasn't there. The instinct on a proof of concept is to polish what exists. Going back to first principles and mapping the full set of user states is what turned this from an execution engagement into a definition one.

  2. 02

    Split the experience into three driving states with distinct interaction models. Separated in situ, in motion, and parked — voice-driven confirmation while moving, touch-driven map search and payment when stopped — which became the structural spine of the whole concept.

  3. 03

    Drove the payment-security discovery with the development team. Resolved how CarPlay and Android Auto each handle stored payment methods before it could break the design, rather than discovering the constraint in build.

  4. 04

    Designed the in-vehicle interface around a driver's actual decision. Reduced the screen to price, ETA to destination, and a garage/street toggle, with reserve and navigate as the only two primary actions — glanceable at speed.

  5. 05

    Built an interactive InVision prototype that set the sprint roadmap. Delivered a prototype Honda stakeholders and the dev team used to prioritise features for upcoming sprints, turning a concept review into a build plan.

Decisions and rationale

The load-bearing decision was designing for the worst context rather than the most convenient one. Building the in-motion lane first — voice confirmation, minimal visual load, no free-text entry — meant the parked experience could be richer without the two diverging into different products. Designing the other way round would have produced a touch interface with a degraded voice fallback bolted on.

Validation and its limits

The concept was reviewed with Honda stakeholders and the Parkopedia development team, and the prototype was used to prioritise sprints. It was not tested with drivers, in a simulator or otherwise. Both statements are true and the second qualifies the first.

05

Solution

A complete in-vehicle parking concept covering three driving states, with a payment flow designed against what CarPlay and Android Auto actually permit.

Key interactions. In motion, the interface offers nearby lots and takes voice confirmation — no map manipulation, no payment entry. Parked, it opens up to map search, price and ETA comparison, and touch-confirmed payment. The transition between the two is the product: the same reservation task, handed between modalities as the car's state changes.

Interface design. The infotainment screen was reduced to the three things that make the decision — price, ETA to destination, and a garage/street toggle — with reserve and navigate as the only two primary actions. Everything else was cut on the grounds that a driver reading it is a driver not watching the road.

What was delivered

  • An interactive prototype good enough to drive feature prioritisation for upcoming sprints.

06

Impact and results

Honda stakeholders approved the concept and the Parkopedia partnership, and the prototype became the instrument used to prioritise upcoming sprints.

Business outcome. The engagement resolved the CarPlay / Android Auto payment-security question that the entire proof of concept depended on, and established the in-motion and error-state design that hadn't previously existed. What Honda gained was a complete, buildable definition of the problem and a partnership decision they could make with evidence.

What this did not do. This concept did not ship to drivers as part of this engagement, and this page makes no claim on any Honda parking feature released subsequently.

User feedback.

Measurement gap

  • Concept work has no usage metrics by definition, and inventing proxies for them would be worse than admitting that. The meaningful evidence this project lacks isn't a KPI — it's a single session with a driver.

What worked

Refusing to design forward from an incomplete map. Auditing the in-house designs first and naming what was missing — error states, the entire in-motion experience — reframed the engagement from execution to definition, and that reframe is why the output became a roadmap rather than a demo. Pulling the payment-security discovery forward was the other decision that mattered: on an in-vehicle product, platform security constraints determine what's designable, and finding that out late would have invalidated the flows.

What I'd do differently

I'd get the in-motion flow in front of drivers, even crudely — a static rig or a driving simulator would have tested whether voice confirmation is genuinely glanceable or just looks that way on a desk. I'd also write an explicit safety position into the deliverable rather than holding it implicitly: designing a payment transaction for someone operating a vehicle deserves a stated stance on what the interface will and won't allow in motion, defensible to more than the product team.

What I learned

Context is a design constraint with more force than any brand or platform requirement. The same task — reserve a parking space — is three different products depending on whether the driver is approaching, moving, or stopped, and the discipline of designing for the worst context rather than the most convenient one is what made this concept survive contact with engineering.
