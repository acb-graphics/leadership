Honda R&D Innovations wanted a proof of concept for finding and paying for parking from inside the vehicle, built on their auto platforms in partnership with Parkopedia.

01

Situation

Honda's in-house design team had already produced a set of designs — but they covered the version of the problem where everything goes right.

In a car, almost nothing about the problem is that simple.

Why it had stalled

  • Only the happy path existed. Error states weren't designed, which in an in-vehicle context means the interface has no answer at exactly the moments it matters.
  • The in-motion state hadn't been considered at all. Searching and paying while the car is moving is a fundamentally different design problem from doing it parked — and it was simply absent from the existing work.
  • Payment security was an unknown across two platforms. CarPlay and Android Auto handle stored payment methods differently, and nobody had established what either would permit before designing against it.
02

Objective

Turn a partial happy-path concept into a complete, technically viable in-vehicle experience — and produce something Honda stakeholders could evaluate as a real product decision rather than a demo.

My role: Lead Product Designer. I owned the flow architecture, the in-vehicle interface design, and the InVision prototype, working with Honda's in-house design team and Parkopedia's development team.

Timeframe: To be confirmed.

What success looked like

  • Full coverage of driving states: in situ, in motion, and parked — each with its own interaction model.
  • Error states and edge cases designed, not deferred.
  • A payment flow validated against how CarPlay and Android Auto actually secure stored payment methods.
  • An interactive prototype good enough to drive feature prioritization for upcoming sprints.
03

Actions

  1. 01

    Audited the existing in-house designs and found what wasn't there. Analyzed Honda's internal design team's work, identified that error states and the in-motion experience were entirely unaddressed, and went back to first principles to map the full set of user states.

  2. 02

    Split the experience into three driving states with distinct interaction models. Separated in situ, in motion, and parked — voice-driven confirmation while moving, touch-driven map search and payment when stopped — which became the structural spine of the whole concept.

  3. 03

    Drove the payment-security discovery with the development team. Worked through how CarPlay and Android Auto each handle stored payment methods, resolving the project's biggest unknown before it could break the design.

  4. 04

    Designed the in-vehicle infotainment interface around a driver's actual decision. Reduced the screen to price, ETA to destination, and a garage/street toggle, with reserve and navigate as the only two primary actions — glanceable at speed.

  5. 05

    Built an interactive InVision prototype that set the sprint roadmap. Delivered a prototype Honda stakeholders and the dev team used to prioritize features for upcoming sprints, turning a concept review into a build plan.

04

Impact

Established the in-motion and error-state design that didn't previously exist — the concept went from a happy path to a complete, buildable experience across three driving states.

The prototype became the prioritization instrument for upcoming sprints, and the concept became the design roadmap Honda and Parkopedia continued building on.

What it delivered

  • Resolved the CarPlay / Android Auto payment-security question, unblocking the flow that the entire proof of concept depended on.
  • Honda stakeholders approved the concept and the Parkopedia partnership.

05

Reflection

What worked

Refusing to design forward from an incomplete map. The instinct on a proof of concept is to polish what exists; auditing the in-house designs first and naming what was missing — error states, the entire in-motion experience — reframed the engagement from execution to definition, and that reframe is why the output became a roadmap rather than a demo. Pulling the payment-security discovery forward was the other decision that mattered: on an in-vehicle product, platform security constraints determine what's designable, and finding that out late would have invalidated the flows.

What I'd refine

The concept was validated with stakeholders and the development team, not with drivers. For an interface used at speed, that's the gap I'd close first — even a static-rig or simulator session would have tested whether the in-motion voice confirmation is genuinely glanceable or just looks like it on a desk. I'd also want a clear position on the safety question written into the deliverable rather than held implicitly: designing a payment transaction for someone operating a vehicle deserves an explicit stance on what the interface will and won't allow in motion, and that stance should be defensible to more than the product team.

What I learned

Context is a design constraint with more force than any brand or platform requirement. The same task — reserve a parking space — is three different products depending on whether the driver is approaching, moving, or stopped, and the discipline of designing for the worst context rather than the most convenient one is what made this concept survive contact with engineering.
