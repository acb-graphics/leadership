01

Project overview

Design lead on an accessibility audit of an app built by another agency, working with a developer on the technical track and with Carnival's product manager on sprint sequencing.

The output was a documented audit and remediation plan — a PDF of prioritised recommendations handed to Carnival to fold into their own sprints. Implementation sat with their team, which shaped the whole approach: a recommendation that couldn't be scoped, sequenced and costed by someone else was a recommendation that wouldn't get done.

Team, tools & scope

  • Team. One developer on the technical track, Carnival's product manager on sequencing.
  • Tools. Shared remediation spreadsheet as the working backlog;
  • Methods. Heuristic audit against AA conformance criteria, component-level blast-radius documentation, split development/visual remediation tracks, sequencing by usage frequency.
  • Scope. 3 component categories audited — buttons, menu navigation, image and text contrast · 5 user scenarios covered — new user, returning user, pre-board, onboard, connectivity errors · individual issues logged
02

Problem statement

The app did not meet accessibility conformance requirements, and it had been designed and built by a different agency.

Business context. This app is the guest experience — reservations, chat, shore excursions, purchases, and the pre-sailing countdown. An accessibility failure here isn't a marketing-site inconvenience; it's a guest unable to book a table or find their excursion while on a ship.

Why it mattered. AA conformance doesn't accept partial credit, and remediation had to happen inside an architecture nobody on our side had authored. The real constraint was implementability: every recommendation had to be something Carnival's own engineers could schedule against an existing roadmap.

Why it was hard

  • Inherited system, no authorship. The app came from another agency, so every recommendation had to work around existing architecture and component decisions rather than replace them.
  • A compliance bar, not a design opinion. Recommendations had to meet the AA conformance level — "improved" wasn't a passing grade.
  • The real use environment couldn't be simulated at a desk. Guests use this app onboard a ship with unreliable connectivity, so error and offline states had to be treated as accessibility surfaces rather than technical footnotes.

User pain points

Failing contrast on primary actions — cancel, continue — meant the controls carrying every booking and purchase were the hardest to see. Menu navigation used five different brand colours for icons, adding visual noise to the app's main wayfinding surface. And text sitting over photography in the countdown, home and shore-excursion screens failed contrast at exactly the moments the app was doing its most emotional work.

03

Research and discovery

This was a heuristic audit — run against the standard rather than with the people the standard exists for.

Method. Every component was evaluated against AA conformance criteria across its full set of states — button default, active and inactive; menu profile, copyright and icon text; image and text contrast — and each failure was documented alongside every flow in which that component appeared.

Blast-radius documentation. The audit recorded not just the fix but its reach: a single button style implicated reservation flows, chat, purchase flows, shore excursion filters and pop-up modals. That mapping is what let engineering scope the true cost of each change rather than discovering it mid-sprint.

Environmental conditions. Onboard connectivity is unreliable, so error and offline states were audited as first-class surfaces rather than skipped — those are the moments a guest most needs the interface to work, and they're the states most audits omit.

Testing with guests: none. No guests with disabilities took part. Conformance and usability are not the same thing, and this audit establishes the first, not the second.

Standard version:

Key findings

  • The failures were concentrated in shared components. A handful of button and menu styles accounted for failures across nearly every flow — which meant a small number of fixes carried disproportionate value.
  • Brand colour was the root cause of most contrast failures. The palette that made the app recognisable was the same palette failing the ratio, so remediation was a brand negotiation as much as a technical one.
  • Error and offline states had never been audited. The states most specific to using an app at sea were the least examined.

04

Design process

  1. 01

    Split the audit into development and visual tracks from the start. Separated screen-reader hierarchy and semantic structure from contrast and sizing, so each set of fixes went to the team equipped to make them instead of arriving as one undifferentiated list.

  2. 02

    Documented every failing element with its full blast radius across the app. For each component, recorded not just the fix but every flow it appeared in — so engineering could scope the true cost of each change rather than discovering it mid-sprint.

  3. 03

    Built a shared remediation spreadsheet with the development team. Logged every recommendation in one artifact the dev team worked from directly, turning an audit document into a working backlog.

  4. 04

    Iterated recommendations until they passed, not until they looked better. The menu profile card went through three passes before landing on sufficient contrast — a compliance bar means iterating against a number, not against taste.

  5. 05

    Sequenced remediation by usage frequency rather than audit order. Worked with the product manager to order fixes by how often guests actually hit each surface, so the highest-traffic failures were scheduled first instead of working through the document top to bottom.

Decisions and rationale

Documenting blast radius alongside each fix was the decision that determined whether this work mattered. An accessibility audit that lists failures is easy to produce and easy to shelve; listing every flow a failing component touched made the cost of each change visible, which is what let a client engineering team schedule it against competing priorities rather than defer it indefinitely.

What got in the way

No authorship of the system being fixed, and no control over whether the fixes shipped. Both pushed the work toward sequencing and scoping: the value I could add wasn't in identifying failures — a tool can do that — but in ordering them so someone else could act.

05

Solution

A documented, AA-conformant component set and a sequenced remediation backlog, delivered as a single audit-and-recommendations document Carnival could fold into their sprints.

Design system elements. The substantive output was a conformant component vocabulary: button styles for default, active and inactive states, with contrast-checked replacements for each; a unified menu icon treatment replacing five competing brand colours with a single tone; and a specified overlay treatment for text on photography (black at 30% alpha) that made the countdown, home and shore-excursion screens legible without re-shooting the imagery.

Visual design choices. Almost every fix traded brand vividness for legibility — teal to deep green on cancel, bright green to a darker green on continue, five icon colours to one. The rationale was consistent: on a compliance project the palette serves the ratio, not the other way round, and a documented replacement is easier to defend to a brand team than a request to "darken it a bit."

Format. A shared remediation spreadsheet the development team worked from directly, plus the audit document itself — a backlog rather than a report requiring translation.

What was delivered

  • A complete audit across buttons, navigation, and image/text contrast, with every failing state documented.
  • Recommendations split into development and visual tracks so each could be routed to the right team.
  • Coverage of five real scenarios — new user, returning user, pre-board, onboard, and connectivity errors.
  • A documented, AA-conformant component set Carnival could build on after the engagement.

06

Impact and results

The engagement delivered a full audit and a prioritised remediation plan, handed to Carnival to fold into their sprints. Implementation and any subsequent conformance assessment sat with their team.

Business outcome. What Carnival received was a conformant component set they could build against and a backlog ordered by guest impact rather than by document order — turning a one-off audit into a durable standard, provided their team ran it. I'd rather state the deliverable precisely than claim an outcome I wasn't in a position to verify.

User feedback.

Being precise about this one

  • An earlier version of this page said the app "passed its accessibility assessment." That overstated my part: the deliverable was an audit and a set of recommendations, and whether the app was subsequently assessed and passed isn't something I implemented or can evidence. The accurate claim is the audit and the plan — which is still the substantive contribution.

What worked

Documenting the blast radius alongside each fix. An accessibility audit that lists failures is easy to produce and easy to ignore; listing every flow a failing component appeared in made the cost of each change visible, which is what gives recommendations a chance of being scheduled rather than shelved. Treating error and offline states as accessibility surfaces was the other decision that mattered — those are exactly the moments when a guest at sea most needs the interface to work, and they're routinely skipped.

What I'd do differently

I'd include guests with disabilities in the work rather than auditing on their behalf. Conformance and usability aren't the same thing, and a heuristic pass tells you the app meets a ratio, not that it's navigable with a screen reader in a corridor at sea. I'd also build a governance step into the handoff — and, knowing what I know now, I'd negotiate a follow-up checkpoint into the engagement so the recommendations don't simply disappear into someone else's backlog with no readout.

What I learned

Inheriting another team's system changes the work fundamentally. The design question stopped being "what's the best solution" and became "what's the best solution that can be implemented here, at this cost, in this order" — and the value I added was as much in sequencing and scoping as in the fixes themselves. That's also the skill that transfers most directly to leading teams rather than projects.
