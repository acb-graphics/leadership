Carnival's iOS and Android app carried the whole guest experience — countdown before sailing, then everything onboard: reservations, chat, shore excursions, purchases.

01

Situation

The app did not meet accessibility conformance requirements, and it had been designed and built by a different agency.

The remediation had to happen inside someone else's architecture, against a standard that doesn't accept partial credit.

Why it was hard

  • Inherited system, no authorship. The app came from another agency, so every recommendation had to work around existing architecture and component decisions rather than replace them.
  • A compliance bar, not a design opinion. Recommendations had to meet the AA conformance level and pass assessment — "improved" wasn't a passing grade.
  • The real use environment couldn't be simulated at a desk. Guests use this app onboard a ship with unreliable connectivity, so the testing environment had to be engineered to reproduce onboard conditions before any recommendation could be trusted.
02

Objective

Audit the full app against AA conformance, and produce remediation the client's engineering team could actually implement inside a legacy codebase — ending in a passed assessment.

My role: Led. Lead Product Designer on the audit and remediation, working directly with a developer on the technical track and with Carnival's product manager on sprint sequencing.

Timeframe: To be confirmed.

What success looked like

  • A complete audit across buttons, navigation, and image/text contrast, with every failing state documented.
  • Recommendations split into development and visual tracks so each could be routed to the right team.
  • Coverage of five real scenarios — new user, returning user, pre-board, onboard, and connectivity errors — not just the default state.
  • A documented, AA-conformant design system Carnival could build on after the engagement.
  • A passing assessment.
03

Actions

  1. 01

    Split the audit into development and visual tracks from the start. Separated screen-reader hierarchy and semantic structure from contrast and sizing, so each set of fixes went to the team equipped to make them instead of arriving as one undifferentiated list.

  2. 02

    Documented every failing element with its full blast radius across the app. For each component — button default, active, inactive — recorded not just the fix but every flow it appeared in (reservations, chat, purchase, shore excursion filters, modals), so engineering could scope the true cost of each change rather than discovering it mid-sprint.

  3. 03

    Built a shared remediation spreadsheet with the development team. Logged every recommendation in one artifact the dev team worked from directly, turning an audit document into a working backlog.

  4. 04

    Engineered a testing environment that reproduced onboard conditions. Insisted the app be tested under real ship connectivity rather than office wifi, which is what surfaced the error and offline states as accessibility problems rather than just technical ones.

  5. 05

    Sequenced remediation by usage frequency and real onboard feedback. Worked with the product manager to run fixes in sprints ordered by how often guests actually hit each surface — so the highest-traffic failures were resolved first rather than working through the audit top to bottom.

Impact

What it delivered

  • A remediation backlog engineering worked from directly, rather than a report requiring translation.

05

Reflection

What worked

Documenting the blast radius alongside each fix. An accessibility audit that lists failures is easy to produce and easy to ignore; listing every flow a failing component appeared in made the cost of each change visible, which is why the recommendations got scheduled instead of shelved. Insisting on real onboard testing conditions was the other decision that mattered — the connectivity-error states would never have been audited from a desk, and those are exactly the moments when a guest most needs the interface to work.

What I'd refine

The audit was heuristic, run against the standard rather than with the people the standard exists for. Compliance and usability aren't the same thing, and I'd have pushed to include guests with disabilities in the onboard testing — passing an assessment tells you the app is conformant, not that it's good to use with a screen reader in a corridor at sea. I'd also want a governance step built in at handoff, so the design system doesn't quietly drift back out of conformance with the next feature release.

What I learned

Inheriting another team's system is a constraint that changes the work fundamentally. The design question stopped being "what's the best solution" and became "what's the best solution that can be implemented here, at this cost, in this order" — and the value I added was as much in sequencing and scoping as in the fixes themselves. That's also the skill that transfers most directly to leading teams rather than projects.
