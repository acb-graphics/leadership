The Systems of
Information Dissemination

A systems study of the complex causes of disinformation and misinformation — mapped, modelled, and turned into intervention points.

Role
Researcher, Designer
Team
Aisha Simpson, Hannah Karunakar, Sylvia MacSpadyen, Umar Shaikh
Client
OCAD University — Strategic Foresight and Innovation
Type
Research
Deliverables
Report, user interview facilitation and synthesis, Gigamap, presentation

Our topic of interest stems from our own lived experiences surrounding the pandemic. We researched the complex subject of vaccine mandates and partisanship in North America as part of the Understanding Systems class, synthesizing our findings into a report and Gigamap.

01

The question

How did misinformation become so widespread — and who benefits from keeping it that way?

Lockdowns, protests and "fake news" let us witness the destabilization of democracy in one of the most polarizing times in history. Rather than treating misinformation as a content problem, we set out to analyze it as a system — one with actors, incentives, and feedback loops that reward its spread.

Two findings organized everything that followed: those in power seek to maintain it, and those in power have no incentive to change.

Why systems, not content

  • Misinformation persists because the system rewards it — fact-checking alone treats the symptom.
  • Four actor groups shape what circulates: traditional media, social media, experts, and government.
  • Public confidence in experts declined measurably across 2021, deepening the problem.
Layer 01 — Litany
What we see on the surface
Vitriol. Mistrust of medical experts. Global-scale pandemics. Polarization. The visible, reported events that dominate the news cycle.
Layer 02 — Systemic
The structures underneath
Historical systematic oppression. Unclear and confusing communication. Inconsistent messaging between institutions that were meant to align.
Layer 03 — Worldview
The beliefs that hold it in place
News is biased. Mistakes were made in relation to medicine, and medicine can be harmful. Distrust in government and in science.
Layer 04 — Myth
The story we tell ourselves
All people are equally cared for by public health. More informed equals more power. Autonomy challenged. Lived experience, history and culture are valued expert opinions.

02

Methods

We combined five research methods, deliberately mixing distanced observation with lived experience.

Because our topic came directly out of our own pandemic experiences, autoethnography wasn't a side note — it was a way of naming our position in the system we were studying rather than pretending to sit outside it.

My role

  • User interview facilitation and synthesis across the semi-structured interviews.
  • Co-design of the Gigamap and its narrative structure.
  • Report writing and the final presentation.

Secondary research

Existing literature on media systems, polarization, and public trust — the baseline the rest of the work was tested against.

Netnography

Observing how misinformation actually moves through online communities, in their own settings and language.

Semi-structured interviews

Conversations that let participants define what mattered, rather than confirming a hypothesis we'd already written.

Autoethnography

Our own lived experience of the pandemic, treated as data and examined with the same rigour as everything else.

Co-creative workshop

Shaped under Afrofuturist themes — using speculative framing to surface futures the group couldn't otherwise articulate.

Synthesis

All of it pulled into a single Gigamap and report, structured as actors → interaction → path forward.

03

Findings

At the centre of the map sat power, money and control — and four actor groups circling it.

Traditional media, social media, experts and government each hold different incentives, but the overlaps are where misinformation accelerates: the business of academia, how parties stay powerful, media escalation, and the attention economy. Information stops being a public good and becomes a vehicle.

We traced two case studies through the map to test it — a New York Times disinformation incident, and a COVID misinformation post going viral — following each from information creation to how it ultimately affects users.

Where the loops close

  • The business of academia — funding pressure shaping what expertise gets published.
  • How parties stay powerful — policy-making with an agenda, and partisanship as strategy.
  • Media escalation — editorial influence competing for clicks and audience.
  • The attention economy — data sold to advertisers, users bracketed and re-targeted.

04

The path forward

A complex system has no single lever — so we mapped intervention points instead of solutions.

Using Meadows' leverage points and a Three Horizons model, we identified where interventions could realistically change system behaviour, and split them across two scales: what societies can change, and what individuals can. The recommendations require the system to transform — but each starts with an individual intervention that unlocks it.

Framing tools used

  • Three Horizons — mapping the current state, the transition, and the preferred future.
  • The Intervention Wheel — leverage points ordered by depth of systemic impact.
  • Societal and individual scales, deliberately paired rather than ranked.
1

Separate money from information and decision making

Remove financial incentive from the actors shaping public information — including how elections and government officials are funded.

2

Empower diverse and local voices to emphasize a pluriverse

Move toward a pluriverse rather than a single dominant paradigm: diverse boardrooms, broadcasters, reporters, and amplified local journalism.

3

Inject transparency into technology and social media companies

Track the information generation and attention-economy causal loops. Once it's developed, surface what is amplifying the headlines whose users so easily identify bad actors and echo chambers.

4

Reduce polarization through citizen assemblies and subordinate goals

Encourage grassroots dialogue groups, citizens' assemblies, and perspective-taking to reduce conflict by driving superordinate goals — fostering empathy among racial, ethnic, religious and ideological divides.
