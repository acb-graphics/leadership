Case Study — Unified 10-Foot Experience

NBC

An early unification concept for NBCUniversal's OTT brands — and what I learned when it went back to the drawing board.

NBC's channels each served a different audience with a different identity — News, MSNBC, Today, and more — and each behaved as its own product. The network wanted one place a viewer could surf all of it.

01

Project overview

Design lead on the engagement, partnering directly with NBC's in-house design team on site at 30 Rockefeller Plaza.

I ran the on-site working sessions, owned the wireframes and functionality sign-off, and led the translation of NBC's brand identity into the 10-foot experience. The unusual condition of this project was that two design organisations were co-authoring one artifact, so my role was as much about making joint decisions durable as about making them.

Team, tools & scope

  • Team.
  • Tools.
  • Methods. On-site collaborative whiteboarding, information architecture, asset and API specification, brand translation for broadcast, remote-driven prototyping.

02

Problem statement

Build one TV experience where a viewer could surf every NBC brand without downloading anything channel-specific, while each brand kept its own character intact.

Business context. NBC had a brand transformation underway internally. What it didn't have was a structure that could hold all of it in one place — and the structure had to anticipate brands and formats that hadn't been defined yet.

Why it mattered. The alternative was a portfolio of separate apps, each requiring its own download and its own discovery moment. Consolidation was the whole strategic point; the design question was whether consolidation could happen without flattening the brands into each other.

Why it was hard

  • Distinct identities, shared shell. Multiple channels serving different personas had to stay recognisably themselves inside one app, which pulled brand differentiation and navigational consistency in opposite directions.
  • It had to scale to content that didn't exist. The architecture couldn't be fitted to the current channel lineup; it had to accommodate future brands and formats nobody could specify yet.
  • Two design organisations, one artifact. The work ran jointly with NBC's in-house design team, so decisions had to survive both agency execution and internal brand governance.

User pain points

The viewer problem was fragmentation: finding NBC content meant knowing which brand produced it and having that brand's app already installed.

03

Research and discovery

No primary user research was run on this engagement. Discovery was internal — conducted with NBC's design team, its brand guidelines, and the technical constraints of the platforms.

Saying that plainly matters more than dressing it up. The architecture was derived from three inputs: what NBC's in-house designers knew about each brand's audience and editorial rhythm, what the brand transformation already specified, and what the backend could actually serve. Those are legitimate inputs, but they are not the same as evidence from viewers — and the reflection below traces a direct line from that absence to how the project ended.

Competitive context.

What discovery established

  • Each brand had a different editorial rhythm. News, MSNBC and Today didn't publish or surface content on the same cadence, which is why a single shelf structure with fixed tab order wouldn't hold across all of them.
  • Asset sizing was the real constraint. Aspect ratios and shelf types determined what the backend could deliver, which meant the information architecture was bounded by content operations, not by design preference.
  • Broadcast conditions govern legibility. Brand colour that works on a monitor can fail for broadcast safety and eye strain at viewing distance, so brand fidelity was constrained by the physics of a television.
04

Design process

  1. 01

    Ran on-site working sessions with NBC's in-house design team at 30 Rock. Whiteboarded the architecture in the room with the client's own designers rather than presenting to them remotely, which is what made joint decisions stick on a project with two design organisations.

  2. 02

    Locked asset sizing and shelf architecture before any high-fidelity work began. Drove aspect ratios and shelf types — Today's Highlights, Continue Watching, Channels — to a decision early, so NBC's backend team could load assets and the development team could build the API against a fixed spec instead of a moving one.

  3. 03

    Designed a six-area architecture built to absorb brands that didn't exist yet. Structured Watch, Shows, Search, Settings, and the two playback contexts, with Watch branching into per-brand channel and grid views and explicit tab-order rules for each brand's content shelf — so adding a channel was a configuration, not a redesign.

  4. 04

    Translated NBC's brand identity into a broadcast-safe 10-foot system. Adjusted colour for broadcast safety and eye strain, and reworked contrast and sizing for legibility at viewing distance.

  5. 05

    Built a navigable prototype with a physical remote mapped to real key assignments. Delivered something stakeholders could pick up and use across brands, shows, and playback, rather than a click-through — the difference between reviewing a concept and testing one.

Testing and iteration

The prototype was reviewed by NBC's design team throughout and taken into internal stakeholder review at the end. It was not tested with viewers, and stakeholder review is not user validation — the two are described separately here on purpose, because conflating them is what made the eventual outcome a surprise rather than an expected correction.

What got in the way

Designing an architecture for content that hadn't been defined yet. The resolution was to make brand a configuration layer rather than a structural one: shelves, tab order and asset specs were defined generically, so a new channel slotted into existing rules instead of forcing a rearchitecture.

05

Solution

A six-area architecture holding every existing NBC brand under one discoverability layer, delivered as a working prototype driven by a real remote.

Key features. Watch was the surfing surface, branching into per-brand channel and grid views so a viewer could move between News, MSNBC and Today without leaving the app. Shows, Search and Settings sat alongside it, with two distinct playback contexts. Explicit tab-order rules governed each brand's content shelf, which is what let brands look different without navigating differently.

Design system elements. Shelf types, aspect ratios and asset specifications were defined as a shared system rather than per brand — the mechanism that made a new channel a configuration rather than a redesign, and the artifact NBC's backend team built against.

Visual design choices. Brand colour was adjusted for broadcast safety and eye strain; contrast and type sizing were reworked for legibility across a room rather than across a desk. Each brand kept its own colour and typographic character within a shared structural grid.

Prototype. Navigable end to end with a physical remote mapped to real key assignments, so stakeholders held the experience rather than watched it.

What was delivered

  • An information architecture holding every existing brand and extensible to future ones.
  • Brand identity translated into a 10-foot context, broadcast-safe and legible at distance.
  • Asset and API specifications locked early enough to keep NBC's backend and the development team in sync.
  • A working prototype stakeholders could navigate with a remote.

06

Impact and results

The concept went back to the drawing board after internal stakeholder review. It did not ship, and NBCUniversal's later streaming platform was developed separately — this work is not a claim on it.

What the work did produce. A navigable prototype that gave NBC a concrete thing to react to at a moment when the brand transformation was still abstract, and an asset and API specification that aligned two engineering organisations on one spec for the duration of the engagement. The architecture demonstrated that consolidation without brand flattening was structurally possible, which is a finding independent of whether this particular concept was the one adopted.

User feedback.

Being precise about this one

  • An earlier version of this page implied a line from this concept to NBCUniversal's later streaming platform. That connection isn't something I can evidence, so it's been removed. The honest version is more useful anyway: this is a case study about a strong project that didn't ship, and about what I changed in how I work because of it.

What worked

Working in the room at 30 Rock instead of across an agency boundary. On a project where the client's own design team owned the brand identity, co-authoring the architecture with them was the only way decisions survived internal review — a deck delivered from outside would have been received rather than defended. Locking asset sizing before high-fidelity design was the other decision that paid off: it's unglamorous, but it kept NBC's backend and our development team building against the same spec for the duration.

What I'd do differently

I'd have surfaced the deciding stakeholders months earlier. We designed with the design team and reviewed with the design team; the people who ultimately made the call saw the work late, as a finished prototype — which is the most expensive possible moment to disagree. On a brand transformation of that scale I'd now insist on exposing the architecture to the deciding audience while it was still cheap to change, and I'd get viewers in front of it too rather than treating internal fluency as a proxy for audience behaviour.

What I learned

Not every strong project ships, and the useful question is what the work made possible rather than whether the artifact survived. I'd rather have built the thing that clarified the decision than the thing that avoided one — but I took a permanent bias from it toward getting concepts in front of decision-makers early, in whatever fidelity they'll engage with.
