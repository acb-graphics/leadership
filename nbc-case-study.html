Case Study — Unified 10-Foot Experience

One discoverability layer across every NBC brand, designed to scale to channels that didn't exist yet.

NBC's channels each served a different audience with a different identity — News, MSNBC, Today, and more — and each behaved as its own product.

01

Situation

The network wanted a single TV experience where a viewer could surf all of it without downloading anything channel-specific, while every brand kept its own character intact.

NBC had the brand transformation underway internally; what it didn't have was a structure that could hold all of it in one place.

Why it was hard

  • Distinct identities, shared shell. Multiple channels serving different personas had to stay recognizably themselves inside one app, which pulled brand differentiation and navigational consistency in opposite directions.
  • It had to scale to content that didn't exist. The architecture couldn't be fitted to the current channel lineup; it had to accommodate future brands and formats nobody could specify yet.
  • Two design organizations, one artifact. The work ran jointly with NBC's in-house design team, so decisions had to survive both agency execution and internal brand governance.
02

Objective

Define and prototype a unified 10-foot experience that unified NBC's brands under one discoverability layer — and get it into stakeholders' hands as something they could actually navigate with a remote.

My role: Led. Design lead on the engagement, partnering directly with NBC's in-house design team on site at 30 Rockefeller Plaza. I ran the on-site working sessions, owned the wireframes and functionality sign-off, and led the translation of NBC's new brand identity into the 10-foot experience.

Timeframe: To be confirmed.

What success looked like

  • An information architecture holding every existing brand and extensible to future ones.
  • Brand identity translated faithfully into a 10-foot context, broadcast-safe and legible across the room.
  • Asset and API specifications locked early enough that NBC's backend and the development team stayed in sync.
  • A working prototype with a real remote, testable by stakeholders rather than presented to them.
03

Actions

  1. 01

    Ran on-site working sessions with NBC's in-house design team at 30 Rock. Whiteboarded the architecture in the room with the client's own designers rather than presenting to them remotely, which is what made joint decisions stick on a project with two design organizations.

  2. 02

    Locked asset sizing and shelf architecture before any high-fidelity work began. Drove aspect ratios and shelf types — Today's Highlights, Continue Watching, Channels — to a decision early, so NBC's backend team could load assets and the development team could build the API against a fixed spec instead of a moving one.

  3. 03

    Designed a six-area architecture built to absorb brands that didn't exist yet. Structured Watch, Shows, Search, Settings, and the two playback contexts, with Watch branching into per-brand channel and grid views and explicit tab-order rules for each brand's content shelf — so adding a channel was a configuration, not a redesign.

  4. 04

    Translated NBC's new brand identity into a broadcast-safe 10-foot system. Adjusted color for broadcast safety and eye strain, and reworked contrast and sizing for legibility at viewing distance — brand fidelity constrained by the physics of a television, not a monitor.

  5. 05

    Built a navigable prototype with a physical remote mapped to real key assignments. Delivered something stakeholders could pick up and use across brands, shows, and playback, rather than a click-through — the difference between reviewing a concept and testing one.

Impact

Delivered a working 10-foot prototype that NBC took into internal stakeholder review — a navigable, remote-driven experience across brands, shows, and playback.

The concept went back to the drawing board after internal review, and the direction that emerged became Peacock TV.

What it delivered

  • Unified six top-level areas and every existing NBC brand into a single discoverability layer, structured to extend to future channels without rearchitecting.
  • Established the asset and shelf specifications NBC's backend team built against, aligning two engineering-facing organizations on one spec.

05

Reflection

What worked

Working in the room at 30 Rock instead of across an agency boundary. On a project where the client's own design team owned the brand identity, co-authoring the architecture with them was the only way decisions survived internal review — a deck delivered from outside would have been received rather than defended. Locking asset sizing before high-fidelity design was the other decision that paid off: it's unglamorous, but it kept NBC's backend and our development team building against the same spec for the duration.

What I'd refine

The concept went back to the drawing board after internal stakeholder review, and I'd want to have surfaced those stakeholders earlier. We designed with the design team and validated with the design team; the people who ultimately made the call saw it late, as a finished prototype. On a brand transformation of that scale, I'd now insist on exposing the architecture to the deciding audience while it was still cheap to change — a rough concept in front of the right room beats a polished one in front of the wrong one.

What I learned

Not every strong project ships, and the useful question is what the work made possible rather than whether the artifact survived. This prototype gave NBC something concrete to react to, and the reaction is what moved the thinking forward. I'd rather have built the thing that clarified the decision than the thing that avoided one — but I took from it a permanent bias toward getting concepts in front of decision-makers early, in whatever fidelity they'll engage with.
